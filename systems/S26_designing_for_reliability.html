Designing for reliability and operations

Reliability is designed in, then proven by testing. This module does the arithmetic of the nines and of failure domains, then runs the mechanisms: a supervisor that restarts a crashing service, an nginx pool that hides a dead server, a page that degrades instead of failing, a real canary that rolls itself back, a feature flag with a kill switch, metrics with percentiles from a live service, a request followed across two services by its id, a database dropped and restored (and a bad backup caught), and an overloaded server that collapses without load shedding.

Why this matters

A real situation

Every system fails; what separates good ones is that the failures are small, short and survivable. That is a design property, built from redundancy across failure domains, safe deployment, observability, tested recovery and protection from overload.

Most outages follow a change or a feedback loop (retries, queues, cascading timeouts). Engineers who understand deployment safety and overload dynamics prevent the largest class of real incidents.

In interviews "how would you make this highly available?" is judged on numbers and mechanisms: the target, the dependencies, the failure domains, the recovery automation and how you would know it works.

After this module you can
  • Convert nines to downtime, compose availability in series and parallel, and reason about failure domains and N+1 capacity.
  • Design supervision, health-checked failover and graceful degradation, and test the failure paths.
  • Release safely with canaries, automatic rollback, feature flags and backward-compatible database changes.
  • Instrument services with metrics, structured logs and request ids, and diagnose a slow request by percentile and trace.
  • Define RPO and RTO, restore and verify backups, and design DR; explain overload, load shedding and metastable failure.
  • Answer reliability interview questions with arithmetic, mechanisms and rehearsed recovery.

Availability arithmetic and failure domains: what the nines really mean

Must know
Situation

The product wants "five nines". Engineering says it is a lot of work. What does 99.999 percent allow per year, what does it cost, and why does putting two servers in the same rack not give you double the reliability?

You will learn
  • Availability in nines, converted to minutes
  • Series and parallel composition
  • Failure domains and correlated failures
  • Deciding the target from the cost of downtime
Mental model

Availability is the fraction of time a service works. In nines: 99 percent allows 3.7 days of downtime a year, 99.9 percent 8.8 hours, 99.99 percent 52 minutes, 99.999 percent 5 minutes. Components in series (all needed) multiply: three 99.9 percent components give 99.7 percent. Components in parallel (any one is enough) multiply their unavailability: two independent 99.9 percent copies give 1 − 0.001² = 99.9999 percent. That is the mathematical case for redundancy, and it holds only if the failures are independent.

Failures are often correlated: two servers in one rack share a power feed; two in one zone share a network; two in one region share a control plane; two deployed with the same bad release share the bug. A failure domain is the set of things that fail together, so redundancy must span domains (racks, zones, regions) and versions (staged rollouts). Add N+1 (or N+2) capacity so that losing one unit does not overload the rest. The practical process: choose an availability target from what an hour of downtime costs, subtract what your dependencies can offer (you cannot be more available than the cloud region and the DNS provider you rely on), and spend engineering where the budget of unavailability is being used up.

Everyday analogy: Two spare tyres in the same boot do nothing for you if a thief takes the whole boot. Redundancy only protects against the failures that the copies do not share.

The picture
How it works, step by step
  1. Set the target from the businessWhat does an hour of downtime cost, and what do customers tolerate?
  2. List the dependencies and their availabilityThe target cannot exceed what the components below it provide.
  3. Find the failure domainsRack, zone, region, vendor, software version: what dies together?
  4. Add redundancy across domainsCopies in different domains, with N+1 capacity.
  5. Remove humans from the failover pathAutomated detection and failover; humans verify, not perform.

Interview in practice

"The business wants 99.99% availability for checkout. How do you design for it, and what does it cost?"

The question: An availability design question. They want arithmetic, failure domains, dependencies and honesty about cost.

Pause for a minute and say how you would start, then compare with the walkthrough below.

1. Clarify first (what to ask, and why it matters)
  • Is 99.99% measured at the user, per month, and what counts as down? What does an hour of downtime cost? Measure at the user's edge; define success with an SLI; compare the cost of downtime with the cost of the extra nines.
2. Think it through (the reasoning, step by step)
  1. Do the arithmetic. 99.99 percent is about 4 minutes a month, 52 a year: shorter than most deployments and most manual recoveries, so failover and deployment must be automated.
  2. Budget the dependencies. List every dependency in the request path with its real availability (DNS, CDN, payment provider, database, cloud region). If a required dependency offers 99.9 percent, you cannot promise 99.99 without making it optional or redundant.
  3. Remove single points of failure across domains. Load balancers, app servers, databases and caches in at least two zones, N+1 capacity; multi-region if the region's own availability is not enough.
  4. Make failures cheap. Graceful degradation: checkout works without recommendations; fallbacks and cached data; timeouts, retries with backoff and circuit breakers; asynchronous steps through queues.
  5. Make changes safe. Most outages follow changes: canary releases, feature flags, automatic rollback, small batches, backward-compatible database migrations.
  6. Make recovery fast and tested. Automated failover, runbooks, game days, tested backups; detection in under a minute.
  7. State the cost. Roughly doubled infrastructure, more engineering and operational discipline per nine; propose the target the business will pay for and an error budget policy.
3. A strong answer, said out loud

"First the arithmetic: 99.99 percent is about four minutes of downtime a month, shorter than most deployments or manual recoveries, so failover and rollout must be automated. Then I list every dependency in the checkout path with its real availability, because if a required one offers 99.9 percent I either make it optional with a fallback or add redundancy; I cannot promise more nines than my dependencies have.

Then I remove single points of failure across failure domains: balancers, app servers, databases and caches in at least two zones with spare capacity, and a second region if the region's own availability is not enough. I make failures cheap with graceful degradation, timeouts, retries with backoff, circuit breakers and queues; make changes safe with canaries, flags and automatic rollback; and make recovery fast and tested with automated failover and game days. And I would state the cost, roughly twice the infrastructure plus engineering discipline, and agree an error budget so the business chooses the target knowingly."

4. Follow-ups you should expect
What is an error budget?

The allowed unreliability implied by the SLO (for example 0.01 percent): spend it on releases and experiments, and slow down when it is exhausted (the reliability practice module covers this).

Why are two servers in one zone not enough?

They share failure domains (power, network, zone control plane), so the independence assumption in the arithmetic is false.

When is multi-region worth it?

When the cost of regional outages exceeds the cost and complexity of running active-active or fast-failover across regions, or when latency or data residency requires it.

Common slips
  • Promising nines without listing dependencies.
  • Redundancy inside one failure domain.
  • Manual failover in a four-nines design.
Quick check

Answer in your head first, then open it.

How much downtime does 99.99% allow per year?

About 52 minutes.

What is the availability of two independent 99.9% copies in parallel?

99.9999%.

What is a failure domain?

The set of components that fail together, such as a rack, zone, region or software version.

Traps to avoid (3)
  • Counting redundant copies as independent when they share a rack, zone or release.
  • Promising a target higher than the least available required dependency.
  • Designing the failover but never testing it.

Redundancy in practice: supervisors, failover and graceful degradation

Must know
Situation

Processes crash. Servers die. A dependency goes slow. The question is not whether, but what happens next: does something restart the process, does traffic move to a healthy copy, and does the user still get a useful, if reduced, page?

You will learn
  • Supervisors and automatic restart
  • Health-checked failover across copies
  • Graceful degradation instead of failure
  • Why the failover path must be tested
Mental model

Reliable systems assume components fail and arrange for automatic recovery. A supervisor (systemd, a container orchestrator, a process manager) restarts a crashed process, with backoff so that a crash loop does not hammer the machine and an alert when it keeps failing. A load balancer with health checks stops sending traffic to a failed copy and resumes when it recovers (as in the load balancing module). Redundant data needs replication and tested failover (scaling data module).

Graceful degradation keeps the core function working when a part fails: the product page loads without recommendations, search falls back to a simpler ranking, checkout accepts the order and queues the email. Design it deliberately: classify dependencies as critical (must work) or optional, give each optional one a timeout, fallback value and circuit breaker, serve stale cached data when the source is down, and shed load (reject low-priority work) before everything collapses. Then test the failure paths: kill instances in production-like environments (chaos testing, game days). A failover that has never run is a hope.

Everyday analogy: A hospital with a backup generator that switches on automatically, a duty rota so someone replaces a sick nurse, and a paper form for when the computers fail: the service continues, reduced but safe, and the generator is tested monthly.

How it works, step by step
  1. Detect the failure quicklyHealth checks, heartbeats, error-rate alerts; seconds, not minutes.
  2. Restart or replace automaticallyA supervisor restarts the process; an orchestrator replaces the instance.
  3. Route around itThe balancer or client removes the failed copy from rotation.
  4. Degrade instead of failingFallbacks and cached data for optional dependencies; shed low-priority load.
  5. RehearseKill things on purpose and watch what users see.

# A tiny supervisor: restart the service when it dies, with exponential backoff (1, 2, 4 ... seconds, capped).
delay=1; n=0
while [ $n -lt 3 ]; do
  python3 flaky.py >/dev/null 2>&1; n=$((n+1))

Self-healing in thirty lines. The service is built to crash 1.5 seconds after each start. A tiny supervisor restarts it each time it exits, waiting longer after each failure (exponential backoff) so that a crash loop cannot saturate the machine. A request while the service is down fails, but after the restart the next one succeeds without a human. Real supervisors (systemd, Kubernetes) add health checks, restart limits and alerts when restarts keep happening: a service that crashes every minute is up but unhealthy.

# Graceful degradation: a product page needs the product (critical) and recommendations (optional, sometimes down).
import random
rng = random.Random(2)
cache = {'recs:42': ['sock', 'hat', 'scarf']}                   # stale but useful recommendations kept from earlier
def get_recommendations(pid, healthy):
    if not healthy: raise TimeoutError('recommendation service timed out')
    return ['new-1', 'new-2']
def page_naive(pid, healthy):
    return {'product': pid, 'recs': get_recommendations(pid, healthy)}
def page_degraded(pid, healthy):
    page = {'product': pid}
    try: page['recs'] = get_recommendations(pid, healthy)
    except TimeoutError: page['recs'] = cache.get('recs:%d' % pid, [])        # optional dependency: fall back to stale data, never fail the page
    return page
failing = [rng.random() < 0.3 for _ in range(1000)]                # the recommender is down for 30% of requests
naive_ok = deg_ok = 0
for f in failing:
    try: page_naive(42, not f); naive_ok += 1
    except TimeoutError: pass
    page_degraded(42, not f); deg_ok += 1
print('recommendation service failing for %d of 1000 requests' % sum(failing))
print('page that treats it as critical:  %4d of 1000 pages served' % naive_ok)
print('page that degrades gracefully:    %4d of 1000 pages served (some with stale recommendations)' % deg_ok)
EOF

Why classifying dependencies matters. The recommender is optional, but the naive page treats it as critical, so a 30 percent failure rate of a nice-to-have feature becomes a 30 percent failure rate of the whole product page. The degraded version catches the failure, uses stale cached recommendations (or none) and serves every page. The user may see slightly older suggestions; they do not see an error. The same pattern applies to personalisation, ads, analytics, reviews and anything that is not the reason the user came.

Interview in practice

"One of our dependencies keeps failing and takes the product down with it. How do you make the system resilient?"

The question: A resilience-design question. They want classification, fallbacks, timeouts and testing, not just "add retries".

Pause for a minute and say how you would start, then compare with the walkthrough below.

1. Clarify first (what to ask, and why it matters)
  • Is the dependency critical to the user's goal? How does it fail: down, slow or wrong? Slow is worse than down; wrong data is worse than missing data.
2. Think it through (the reasoning, step by step)
  1. Classify the dependency. Critical (the request cannot succeed without it) or optional (the request can succeed without it, perhaps degraded).
  2. Optional: degrade. Timeout shorter than the page's budget, fallback value (stale cache, default, empty), circuit breaker so you stop calling a sick dependency, and a metric that shows how often the fallback is used.
  3. Critical: make it redundant. Multiple instances across failure domains, health-checked failover, capacity headroom, retries with backoff only for idempotent calls; or queue the work and complete it later.
  4. Protect yourself from slowness. Timeouts everywhere, bulkheads (separate pools), load shedding, bounded queues.
  5. Recover automatically. Supervisors, health checks, auto-scaling and replacement, and alerts when the automatic recovery keeps firing.
  6. Test it. Fault injection: kill and slow the dependency in a test environment and in production at low blast radius; observe user-visible behaviour and fix the surprises.
  7. Measure. SLI at the user's edge, dependency error and latency, fallback rate, circuit state.
3. A strong answer, said out loud

"First I classify the dependency: can the request succeed without it? If it is optional, I give it a timeout shorter than the page's budget, a fallback such as stale cached data or a default, and a circuit breaker so we stop hammering it when it is sick, plus a metric for how often the fallback is used. If it is critical, I make it redundant across failure domains with health-checked failover and spare capacity, and use retries with backoff only for idempotent calls, or queue the work to finish later.

I protect against slowness with timeouts everywhere, separate pools so one slow dependency cannot use all the threads, bounded queues and load shedding. Recovery is automatic through supervisors and health checks, with alerts when it keeps firing. Then I test the failure paths by killing and slowing the dependency on purpose, and measure the user-facing success rate rather than the dependency's own health."

4. Follow-ups you should expect
Why is a slow dependency worse than a dead one?

A dead one fails fast and frees resources; a slow one ties up threads and connections in every caller until they fail too.

What is a bulkhead?

Isolating resources (thread pools, connections) per dependency so one failing dependency cannot exhaust everything.

What is chaos engineering?

Deliberately injecting failures (killed instances, added latency) to verify that the system degrades as designed, starting small and controlled.

Common slips
  • Retries as the only answer.
  • Treating every dependency as critical.
  • Never exercising the fallbacks.

What does a supervisor add to a service?

Automatic restart on crash, with backoff so crash loops do not overload the machine.

What is graceful degradation?

Keeping the core function working when an optional part fails, with fallbacks such as stale data.

Why must you test failover paths?

An untested failover usually fails the first time it is needed.

Traps to avoid (3)
  • Restart loops with no backoff and no alert: a service that is "up" but crashing every minute.
  • Fallbacks that are never exercised and silently broken.
  • Redundancy with no spare capacity: losing one copy overloads the rest.
3

Safe deployments: canaries, rollbacks and feature flags

Must know
Situation

Most outages follow a change. A deployment that reaches every user at once turns a mistake into an incident. The goal is to let a bad change hurt as few people as possible for as short a time as possible.

You will learn
  • Rolling, blue-green and canary releases
  • Deciding automatically whether a canary is healthy
  • Feature flags: separating deploy from release
  • Backward-compatible database changes
Mental model

A rolling deployment replaces instances a few at a time (simple, but old and new versions run together and rollback means rolling forward again). Blue-green runs two full environments and switches the router: switching back is instant, at the cost of double capacity and the need to keep the data compatible. A canary sends a small share of real traffic (1 percent, then 5, 25, 100) to the new version while comparing its error rate and latency with the old one, and automatically rolls back when it is worse. Canary plus automation limits the blast radius and the time to detect.

Feature flags separate deploy (code on the servers) from release (users see it): ship the code switched off, enable it for staff, then 1 percent of users, then everyone, and switch it off in seconds if metrics worsen, without redeploying. Flags need hygiene (remove old ones). Database changes must be backward compatible with both the old and new code, done in steps (expand: add the new column; migrate: write both; contract: remove the old one later), because during any rollout both versions run against the same data. Keep changes small and frequent: small changes have small blast radii, are easy to review and easy to revert.

Everyday analogy: Introducing a new recipe at a restaurant: serve it to one table, watch the reactions, then more tables, and keep the old kitchen ready. A feature flag is a menu card you can slip in or out without rebuilding the kitchen.

The picture
How it works, step by step
  1. Ship the new version beside the oldBoth run; the router decides who sees which.
  2. Send a small share to the new version1 percent of requests, chosen at random or by user.
  3. Compare error rate and latency by versionAutomatically, over a window long enough to be meaningful.
  4. Promote or roll back by ruleIf the canary is within tolerance, increase the share; if not, route everything back.
  5. Keep data compatibleExpand, migrate, contract: both versions must work with the schema at every step.

Observability: metrics, logs and traces that answer "what is wrong?"

Must know
Situation

Users report that checkout is slow. The average latency graph looks fine. Forty services are involved. Where do you look first, and what should have been recorded so that the answer takes minutes instead of hours?

You will learn
  • Counters, gauges and histograms (and why the average lies)
  • Percentiles from histogram buckets
  • Structured logs and request ids across services
  • RED and USE, and alerting on symptoms
Mental model

Metrics are numbers sampled over time: counters (requests served: only go up; you look at their rate), gauges (queue depth, memory: go up and down) and histograms (latency distribution in buckets). The average latency hides the pain: 99 requests at 20 ms and one at 2 seconds average 40 ms while one user in a hundred waits two seconds. Track percentiles (p50, p95, p99), computed from histogram buckets, since tail latency is what users and upstream timeouts experience, and in a chain of services the tail compounds. Logs record events in detail; make them structured (JSON with fields) and include a request id (and user or tenant id) so one request can be followed across services. Traces record the timeline of a request across services as nested spans, showing where the time went.

A usable method: RED for services (Rate, Errors, Duration) and USE for resources (Utilisation, Saturation, Errors). Alert on symptoms that users feel (error rate and latency at the edge, measured against an SLO), not on every internal cause (CPU at 80 percent): causes are for dashboards, symptoms are for pages. Keep high-cardinality labels (user id) out of metrics (they explode storage) and in logs and traces. Add deploy markers and version labels so changes line up with graphs. A system you cannot observe, you cannot operate.

Everyday analogy: A car dashboard: the speedometer and warning lights (metrics) tell you something is wrong, the diagnostic computer (traces) says which system, and the service history and sensor logs (logs) say why. Average speed over a trip would never tell you about the five seconds the brakes failed.

  3. Log structured events with a request idOne id travels with the request through every service.

Disaster recovery and overload: restoring from nothing, and surviving too much

Must know
Situation

Someone drops the production table. A region goes dark. Or the opposite problem: you are doing fine until traffic doubles, and the system's own retries finish it off. These are the two ends of reliability: recover from loss, and survive excess.

You will learn
  • RPO and RTO, and what they cost
  • A backup you have restored is the only backup
  • Failover patterns: backup and restore, warm standby, active-active
  • Load shedding and metastable failure
Mental model

Disaster recovery is defined by two numbers: RPO (recovery point objective: how much recent data you can afford to lose) and RTO (recovery time objective: how long you can be down). Backups give an RPO of the time since the last backup and an RTO of the restore time, which is usually much longer than people assume; continuous archiving / replication shrinks the RPO to seconds; a warm standby or active-active second site shrinks the RTO to minutes or seconds, at a cost in money and complexity. The rule: a backup that has not been restored is a hope, not a backup: test restores regularly, time them, keep copies in another failure domain and account, protect them from the same credentials that can delete production, and keep some immutable or offline.

Overload is the other failure. A server has a capacity; beyond it, an unprotected system does not serve capacity requests per second, it serves fewer: queues grow, latency exceeds client timeouts, clients retry, and the server spends its effort on answers nobody is waiting for (congestion collapse). A metastable failure is when this retry load keeps the system down even after the original trigger has gone. The cure is load shedding (reject excess requests early and cheaply, with 503 or 429 and a Retry-After), bounded queues, timeouts propagated downstream, client-side backoff and retry budgets, prioritising important traffic, and autoscaling with headroom (it takes minutes, so it does not replace shedding). Rehearse both with game days and load tests.

Everyday analogy: Disaster recovery is the fire drill and the offsite copy of the ledger: you only know it works if you have run it. Overload is a restaurant on a rush night: a host who turns some people away at the door keeps the kitchen serving fast; a host who seats everyone makes every meal arrive cold.

The picture
How it works, step by step
  1. Set RPO and RTO with the businessWhat data loss and what downtime are acceptable, and what does each cost?
  2. Back up to another failure domainDifferent account, different region, some copies immutable.
  3. Restore on a schedule and time itThe measured restore time is your real RTO.
  4. Protect the front doorBounded queues, shedding with 503 and Retry-After, priorities.
  5. Rehearse bothDisaster drills and overload tests before the real thing.

python3 - <<'EOF'
# Overload in virtual time: a server can finish 100 requests per second. Clients give up after 1 second and RETRY once.
# "goodput" = requests answered before the client gave up.
def simulate(offered_rate, shed, seconds=30, capacity=100, timeout=1.0, max_queue=50):
    queue = []                                    # arrival times of waiting requests (FIFO)
    good = bad = shed_count = 0
    carry = 0.0; served_budget = 0.0
    for tick in range(seconds * 100):            # 10 ms ticks
        now = tick / 100
        arrivals = offered_rate / 100
        n = int(arrivals + carry); carry = arrivals + carry - n
        for _ in range(n):
            if shed and len(queue) >= max_queue: shed_count += 1; continue        # reject early: cheap
            queue.append(now)
        served_budget += capacity / 100
        while served_budget >= 1 and queue:
            served_budget -= 1; arrived = queue.pop(0)                             # the server works on the oldest request first
            if now - arrived <= timeout: good += 1
            else: bad += 1                                                         # finished too late: the client already left; work wasted
    return good / seconds, bad / seconds, shed_count / seconds
print('capacity: 100 requests/s.  client timeout: 1 s.')
print('offered   WITHOUT shedding              WITH shedding (queue limit 50)')
print('load/s    useful/s  wasted/s            useful/s  wasted/s  rejected/s')
for rate in (50, 90, 100, 150, 300):
    g1, b1, _ = simulate(rate, False); g2, b2, r2 = simulate(rate, True)
    print('%6d    %7.0f  %7.0f             %7.0f  %7.0f  %9.0f' % (rate, g1, b1, g2, b2, r2))
EOF

Congestion collapse in numbers. Up to the server's capacity (100 per second) both versions serve everything. Beyond it, the unprotected server keeps accepting requests, its queue grows without limit, every request waits longer than the client's one-second timeout, and the server spends all its effort on answers nobody is waiting for: useful throughput falls towards zero while the machine is 100 percent busy. The server with a bounded queue rejects the excess immediately and keeps delivering about its full capacity of useful answers, whatever the offered load. Rejecting some requests quickly is what allows the rest to succeed.

Interview in practice

"Design disaster recovery for a payments database, and explain what happens when traffic doubles unexpectedly."

The question: A two-part reliability question: recovery from loss and survival under overload. They want numbers and mechanisms.

Pause for a minute and say how you would start, then compare with the walkthrough below.

1. Clarify first (what to ask, and why it matters)
  • What are the RPO and RTO the business requires, and what is the data volume? Payments usually need RPO near zero and an RTO of minutes; that dictates synchronous replication and a hot standby.
2. Think it through (the reasoning, step by step)
  1. Define RPO and RTO. Payments: RPO of seconds or zero, RTO of minutes. Backups alone (RPO hours, RTO hours) are not enough.
  2. Choose the architecture. Synchronous replica in another zone (zero loss for a zone failure), asynchronous replica in another region (seconds of loss, survives a region), automated failover with fencing, continuous WAL archiving for point-in-time recovery.
  3. Backups. Regular base backups plus archived WAL to another account and region, some immutable or offline copies, encryption with keys held separately; protect against the compromised-credentials and operator-error scenarios.
  4. Test it. Automated restore tests with checksums and row counts, timed; quarterly failover and region-evacuation drills; measure the real RPO and RTO and report them.
  5. Overload part. Autoscaling takes minutes and databases do not scale instantly; so protect with rate limits per client, bounded queues and load shedding at the edge (503/429 with Retry-After), priority for critical operations (payments over reporting), timeouts and deadlines propagated, client backoff with retry budgets, and a degraded mode that disables expensive optional features.
  6. Avoid metastable failure. Do not let retries amplify: cap retries, circuit breakers; after an incident, ramp traffic back gradually.
  7. Operations. Runbooks, dashboards for saturation and queue depth, capacity reviews, load tests at 2x peak, game days.
3. A strong answer, said out loud

"First the objectives: for payments I would want an RPO of seconds or zero and an RTO of minutes, which backups alone cannot give. So: a synchronous replica in another zone, an asynchronous replica in another region, automated failover with fencing, and continuous archiving of the write-ahead log for point-in-time recovery, plus base backups copied to another account and region with some immutable copies and separate encryption keys. And I would restore them automatically and time it, and rehearse failover and region evacuation regularly, because the measured RTO is the only one that counts.

When traffic doubles unexpectedly, autoscaling takes minutes and a database cannot scale instantly, so the system must protect itself: rate limits per client, bounded queues, and load shedding at the edge with 503 and Retry-After, priority for payments over reporting, timeouts and deadlines propagated downstream, client backoff with retry budgets and circuit breakers so retries do not create a metastable failure, and a degraded mode that switches off expensive optional features. I would load test at twice the peak and hold game days."

4. Follow-ups you should expect
What is point-in-time recovery?

Restoring a base backup and replaying archived write-ahead log up to a chosen moment (such as just before a bad command), giving an RPO of seconds.

Why is your restore time longer than you think?

Restore must copy the data, rebuild indexes, replay logs and verify, often sequentially and over a network: measure it with production-sized data.

What is metastable failure?

A state where a feedback loop (typically retries) keeps the system overloaded after the original trigger is gone, so it stays down until load is reduced.

Common slips
  • Backups never restored.
  • Autoscaling as the only answer to overload.
  • Backups stored with the same credentials as production.
Quick check

Answer in your head first, then open it.

What do RPO and RTO mean?

RPO: how much data you can lose; RTO: how long you can be down.

Why test restores?

A backup that has never been restored may be unusable, and the restore time is your real RTO.

What does load shedding do?

Rejects excess requests early so the accepted ones finish quickly and useful throughput stays at capacity.

Traps to avoid (3)
  • Backup jobs that report success but whose files are corrupt or incomplete.
  • Backups in the same account and region as production: one mistake or one outage takes both.
  • Unbounded queues and unlimited retries: the system collapses under the load it was supposed to absorb.
