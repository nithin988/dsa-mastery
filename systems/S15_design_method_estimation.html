Module S15 of 29System design core

The design method and estimation

System design is not about knowing the one right architecture. It is a repeatable way of thinking: pin down the requirements, do the arithmetic that shows how big the problem is, sketch the simplest design that fits, then go deep where it is hard and explain your trade-offs. This module teaches that method and the numbers that make it work.

5 lessons2 labs2 animations6 interview questionsabout 4 to 5 hours

Why this matters

A real situation

An interviewer says "design a URL shortener" and falls silent. Candidates who start drawing boxes at once usually design for the wrong problem. Candidates who ask three questions and compute 12,000 requests per second know within two minutes whether one database is enough.

At work the same skill decides whether a new feature needs a cache, a queue or just a bigger server, before anyone writes code. Rough numbers prevent expensive wrong turns.

After this module you can
  • Follow a six-step design framework and split the time sensibly.
  • Separate functional from non-functional requirements and ask the questions that matter.
  • Use the latency numbers every engineer should know, and measure them yourself.
  • Turn users and actions into requests per second, storage and servers.
  • Explain trade-offs and say what would change your design.

Before you start

Comfortable with the networking and storage ideas from the earlier modules; each concept used here is re-explained briefly.

Calculation examples use bash arithmetic and python3. The latency-measuring script needs only python3. Numbers you measure on your machine will differ.

Your practice shell

Scratch folder: mkdir -p ~/dsg && cd ~/dsg.

The lessons

1

The six steps of a design discussion

Must know
Situation

"Design a chat system." You have 45 minutes and an open-ended problem. Where do you start, and how do you avoid wandering?

You will learn
  • The six steps and rough time for each
  • Why start simple
  • Where interviewers steer you
Mental model

Use the same six steps every time: requirements (what and how well), estimate (how big), API and data (the contract and the records), high-level design (the simplest boxes that work), deep dive (the one or two hard parts) and trade-offs (bottlenecks, failures, alternatives). Order matters: each step produces the facts the next one needs. Start with the simplest design that meets the requirements and add a component only when a number or requirement forces it.

Everyday analogy: An architect does not start with the wallpaper: first the brief, then the size of the plot, then the floor plan, then the tricky structural parts.

Press play, or step with the buttons, the slider or the arrow keys.

Quick check

Answer in your head first, then open it.

Why estimate before drawing the architecture?

The numbers decide the design: 100 requests per second and 100,000 need very different solutions.

What should the first design contain?

The simplest set of components that meets the requirements; add more only when a number or requirement demands it.

Traps to avoid (2)
  • Jumping straight to microservices and Kafka before knowing the scale is the most common mistake.
  • Spending the whole time on one box; leave time for trade-offs and failure cases.
2

Requirements: what, how well, how big

Must know
Situation

The prompt is one line. The right design depends on facts nobody has told you yet.

You will learn
  • Functional versus non-functional
  • Questions that change the design
  • Stating assumptions out loud
Mental model

Functional requirements are features: what a user can do. Non-functional requirements are qualities: latency, availability, consistency, durability, scale and cost. Non-functional ones usually drive the architecture: "always available" and "never lose a message" point to replication, while "reads must be instant" points to caching. Ask questions that change the design (read-heavy or write-heavy? one region or global? must it be strongly consistent?) and when the interviewer lets you decide, state your assumption clearly and move on.

Everyday analogy: Ordering a car: what it does (carry five people) versus how well (fuel use, safety rating, price).

The picture
Quick check

Answer in your head first, then open it.

Give two non-functional requirements.

For example latency, availability, consistency, durability, scalability, cost.

What kind of question is worth asking early?

One whose answer changes the design: read versus write heavy, scale, consistency needs, regions, what is out of scope.

Traps to avoid (2)
  • Asking ten questions with no effect on the design wastes time; ask the ones that matter and assume the rest.
  • Silently assuming scale. Say "I will assume 40 million daily users" so the interviewer can correct you.
3

Numbers every engineer should know

Must know
Situation

Is it fine to call another service for every request? Is a disk read cheap? Without a feel for latencies, design decisions are guesses.

You will learn
  • Orders of magnitude of latency
  • Memory, SSD, network and disk compared
  • Measuring them yourself
Mental model

You do not need exact figures, only orders of magnitude. Reading from memory takes about a hundred nanoseconds; a random read from an SSD about a hundred microseconds; a round trip inside one data centre about half a millisecond; a spinning-disk seek about ten milliseconds; a round trip across continents about a hundred and fifty milliseconds. Each step is roughly 1,000 times slower than the one before. That is why caches exist, why you batch network calls and why you keep data close to users.

Everyday analogy: Getting a fact: from your head (memory), from a book on your desk (SSD), by phoning the next office (data-centre network), by posting a letter overseas (cross-continent).

Measure three of them yourself 
mkdir -p ~/dsg && cd ~/dsg && cat > lat.py <<'EOF'
import time, socket, threading, os
def t(f, n):
    s = time.perf_counter()
    for _ in range(n):
        f()
    return (time.perf_counter() - s) / n * 1e9
d = {i: i for i in range(1000)}
print('memory lookup      %8.0f ns' % t(lambda: d[500], 200000))
open('x.bin', 'wb').write(os.urandom(4096))
def rd():
    with open('x.bin', 'rb') as f:
        f.read()
print('file read, 4 KB   %8.0f ns' % t(rd, 20000))
srv = socket.socket(); srv.bind(('127.0.0.1', 0)); srv.listen()
port = srv.getsockname()[1]
def echo():
    c, _ = srv.accept()
    while True:
        b = c.recv(64)
        if not b:
            break
        c.sendall(b)
threading.Thread(target=echo, daemon=True).start()
cl = socket.create_connection(('127.0.0.1', port))
def rt():
    cl.sendall(b'x'); cl.recv(64)
print('local TCP round trip %6.0f ns' % t(rt, 20000))
EOF
python3 lat.py

Three measurements on your own machine: a dictionary lookup (memory), reading a cached 4 KB file (page cache) and a round trip over the loopback network.

file read, 4 KB       6720 ns
local TCP round trip  69140 ns

Python adds its own overhead, so read the ratios, not the digits. A real network hop between machines is slower again.

Quick check

Answer in your head first, then open it.

Roughly how much slower is a data-centre network round trip than a memory read?

About 5,000 times (0.5 ms against 100 ns); that is why a remote call per request adds up.

Why are caches useful?

They keep hot data in a much faster layer (memory) than the one where it normally lives (disk or another service).

Traps to avoid (2)
  • Quote orders of magnitude, not false precision. "About half a millisecond within a data centre" is right; "0.4837 ms" is not.
  • Hardware improves; the ratios between levels stay roughly similar, which is what design decisions need.
4

Back-of-envelope: users to QPS, storage and servers

Must know
Situation

"40 million users" means nothing until it becomes requests per second, terabytes per year and a number of machines.

You will learn
  • DAU to QPS, average and peak
  • Storage and bandwidth
  • Servers needed with headroom
Mental model

A short chain of multiplications turns users into load: daily users x actions per user = requests per day; divide by 86,400 for the average per second; multiply by 2 to 5 for the peak. Storage is records per day x bytes per record x days kept (times copies if replicated). Servers are peak QPS divided by what one server can handle, with headroom (plan for 50 to 70 percent utilisation). Round aggressively: 86,400 is about 100,000 seconds, and 1 million requests a day is about 12 per second.

Everyday analogy: Planning a restaurant: guests per day, dishes per guest, hours open, and therefore how many cooks you need at the rush.

Quick check

Answer in your head first, then open it.

How do you turn requests per day into requests per second?

Divide by 86,400 (about 100,000 for a quick mental estimate).

Why plan for 60 percent utilisation instead of 100?

Headroom for traffic spikes, failures of other servers and deploys.

Traps to avoid (3)
  • Designing for the average and not the peak; the system fails at the peak.
  • Forgetting replication and indexes when estimating storage: they multiply it.
  • Rounding down when sizing capacity. Always round up.
5

Trade-offs and how to talk about them

Must know
Situation

There is no perfect design. Interviewers (and teammates) want to hear what you chose, what you gave up and what would change your mind.

You will learn
  • Naming the trade-off
  • Bottlenecks and single points of failure
  • Staying open to feedback
Mental model

Every choice trades something: a cache makes reads fast but can serve stale data; replication improves availability but costs consistency or money; a queue smooths spikes but adds delay. A strong answer follows a pattern: say the choice, say what it costs, say when you would choose differently. Finish by hunting for bottlenecks (what saturates first?) and single points of failure (what takes everything down?). When the interviewer pushes back, treat it as new information, not as an attack.

Everyday analogy: Choosing a route: the motorway is faster but tolled, the back roads are free but slow; the right answer depends on what you are in a hurry about.

Quick check

Answer in your head first, then open it.

What is the pattern of a good trade-off statement?

The choice, what it costs, and when you would choose differently.

What is a single point of failure?

A component whose failure takes the whole system down; remove it with redundancy.

Traps to avoid (2)
  • Presenting your design as flawless. Naming its weaknesses first signals seniority.
  • Defending a choice instead of reasoning about the interviewer's challenge.

Interview corner

Start every design answer by stating the framework you will follow. It shows structure and gives the interviewer something to steer.

How do you approach a system design problem you have not seen before?
Shape of a strong answer
  • Clarify requirements (functional and non-functional) and state assumptions.
  • Estimate scale: users, QPS, storage, bandwidth.
  • Define the API and data model, then draw the simplest high-level design.
  • Deep dive into the hardest one or two parts, then discuss bottlenecks, failures and trade-offs.
Follow-ups to expect
  • How do you decide where to go deep?
  • What do you do if the interviewer changes a requirement?
Common mistakes
  • Drawing boxes before asking questions.
  • Over-engineering the first design.

Company angle: Google-style: candidates commonly report that interviewers watch how you handle ambiguity, scale and trade-offs aloud; reported by candidates, not official.

Estimate the requests per second for a service with 10 million daily active users.
Shape of a strong answer
  • State assumptions: say 20 actions per user per day.
  • 10M x 20 = 200M requests per day, divided by about 100,000 s is about 2,000 per second on average; peak 3 to 5 times that, so 6,000 to 10,000.
  • Conclude: a handful of servers behind a load balancer, a database that handles that read and write mix, probably a cache.
Follow-ups to expect
  • How does read/write ratio change the design?
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting the peak factor.
  • Not stating the assumptions.
What latency numbers should every engineer know and why?
Shape of a strong answer
  • Memory about 100 ns, SSD random read about 100 us, data-centre round trip about 0.5 ms, disk seek about 10 ms, cross-continent round trip about 150 ms.
  • Each level is hundreds of times slower, which justifies caching, batching and putting data near users.
Follow-ups to expect
  • How would you use them to decide whether to cache?
Common mistakes
  • Exact digits instead of orders of magnitude.
Functional versus non-functional requirements: give examples and say which usually drives the architecture.
Shape of a strong answer
  • Functional: features such as shorten and redirect. Non-functional: latency, availability, consistency, durability, scale, cost.
  • Non-functional requirements usually drive the architecture: high availability needs replication, low latency needs caching or proximity, scale needs partitioning.
Follow-ups to expect
  • Which would you ask about first?
Common mistakes
  • Listing only features.
How many servers do you need for 30,000 requests per second if one server handles 2,000?
Shape of a strong answer
  • 30,000 / 2,000 = 15 at full load, but plan for 60 percent utilisation: 15 / 0.6 = 25.
  • Add redundancy across failure zones (at least N+1, often spread over three zones) and round up.
Follow-ups to expect
  • What if the load doubles on a holiday?
Common mistakes
  • Sizing for 100 percent utilisation and no failure headroom.
Tell me about a trade-off you made in a design and why.
Shape of a strong answer
  • Name the situation and the options, the criteria (cost, latency, risk) and the choice.
  • State what you gave up and how you mitigated it, with a number if you can (for example a cache that raised hit rate to 95 percent but allowed 60 seconds of staleness).
  • Say what you would revisit as the system grows.
Follow-ups to expect
  • What would make you reverse the decision?
Common mistakes
  • A trade-off story with no cost named.

Company angle: Amazon-style: expect behavioural follow-ups about ownership and customer impact; reported by candidates, not official.

Self-test

Answer in your head first, then open the question.

List the six steps of a design discussion.

Requirements, estimate, API and data, high-level design, deep dive, trade-offs.

How do you get average requests per second from daily requests?

Divide by 86,400 (about 100,000 for mental math).

Why size for peak instead of average?

The system fails at the peak, not the average.

What is a good utilisation target when sizing servers?

About 50 to 70 percent, leaving headroom for spikes and failures.

Which is slower: an SSD random read or a same-data-centre network round trip?

The network round trip (about 0.5 ms versus about 0.1 ms).

What multiplies storage estimates besides record size?

Days kept, replication copies and indexes.

What is a non-functional requirement? Name two.

A quality the system must have, for example latency and availability.

What is the shape of a trade-off statement?

The choice, what it costs, and when you would choose differently.
