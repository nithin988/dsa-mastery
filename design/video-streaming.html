Design video streaming

Looks like a file download problem. It is really a delivery cost problem with a processing pipeline in front.

about 30 minutes1 real experimentsA staple at media and large consumer companies

1The film that froze on the trainThe question

ShopKart launches a small video section: product demos, around 3 minutes each. The first version stores one MP4 per video and serves it from the web server. In the office it works. Then a customer opens a demo on a commuter train and sends a screenshot of a spinner that has been turning for a minute.

The team learns two things at once. Phones have wildly different speeds, and a single file cannot adapt to any of them. And the bandwidth bill for the demos is already bigger than the bill for everything else. The question becomes: how would you do this for the whole internet, the way YouTube or Netflix do?

The interviewer says: Design YouTube or Netflix: upload, process and stream video to millions of viewers.

What they are looking for: A staple design question. They want the upload and processing pipeline, storage, delivery through a CDN and adaptive playback, with numbers.

2What to ask before drawing anythingRequirements

Two minutes of questions is what separates a design from a guess. Write the answers on the board.

  • Video on demand or live? Upload volume, viewers, regions, device types, and are downloads allowed offline? Assume video on demand: 500 hours uploaded per minute, a billion hours watched per day, global audience, all devices.

Functional: upload a video; process it so it plays on any device; stream it with fast start and no freezing; seek anywhere. Non-functional: playback starts in a second or two, quality adapts to the connection, uploads never get lost, and delivery cost stays under control. Assume video on demand, not live.

3The numbers that shape the designEstimates

QuantityEstimate and what it tells you
Upload volume500 hours of video arrive every minute (the scenario's assumption). That is 30,000 hours per hour, or 720,000 hours of source video per day.
Storage per titleThe recorded ladder below stores one 2 hour film in four qualities: 0.21 + 0.68 + 1.57 + 3.87 = 6.3 GB, about 3.15 GB per hour. At 720,000 hours a day that is roughly 2.3 PB of new renditions per day before replication. This is our estimate, not a published figure.
Delivery volumeAssume a billion hours watched per day at an average of 3 Mbps. 3 Mbps is 0.375 MB per second, so one hour is 1.35 GB. Times a billion: about 1.4 exabytes per day. Delivery dwarfs storage.
What it meansStorage is cheap compared with delivery, so we happily store many copies of each title. The design goal is to serve almost every viewer from a cache close to them, and to keep the origin nearly idle.
SegmentsA 2 hour film in 4 second segments is 1,800 segments per rendition. Small enough to cache and retry, large enough to avoid a flood of tiny requests.

1A manifest and the storage cost of a bitrate ladder

What the player and the CDN actually see. The master playlist advertises each rendition with its bandwidth and resolution; the media playlist lists segment files, four seconds each. A two-hour film is 1,800 small files per rendition, served as ordinary static files, which is why a CDN can cache and deliver them. The ladder multiplies storage by about two and a half (all four renditions together), a small price compared with the bandwidth cost of delivery.

4A first design that worksArchitecture

  1. Upload straight to storage, in resumable parts

    The API checks the user and quota, then hands back a presigned URL (a temporary address that lets the client write to the store directly). The client uploads in parts, and if the connection drops it resumes where it stopped. The video bytes never pass through your app servers.

  2. Transcode in parallel into a ladder

    Transcoding means re-encoding the video into other sizes and bitrates. A workflow splits the source into chunks, a pool of workers encodes chunks in parallel into a ladder of 240p, 480p, 720p and 1080p, and the results are cut into segments. If a worker dies, only its chunk runs again.

  3. A manifest tells the player what exists

    A manifest is a small text file that lists every quality and every segment address. The player downloads it first, then asks for segments one by one. The first segment is chosen small and low quality so the picture starts fast.

  4. Serve from a CDN, not from your origin

    A CDN (content delivery network) is a fleet of caches near viewers. The first viewer of a segment causes one fetch from the origin; the next thousand are answered by the edge. Popular new releases can be copied to the edges before anyone asks.

5The hard part: adaptive playback and the cost of deliveryThe hard part

Every interview has one part where the answer is not obvious. Spend your time here.

The player measures how fast the last segment arrived and chooses the quality of the next one. This is adaptive bitrate streaming (ABR). Fast network: climb the ladder. Tunnel: step down before the buffer empties. Because each segment is a separate small request, switching costs nothing.

The second hard part is money. Because delivery is about 1.4 exabytes a day in our estimate, the CDN hit ratio is the biggest cost lever. Short segments adapt faster but create more requests; longer ones are cheaper to serve but react slowly. Say which you picked and why. Newer codecs save bandwidth but not every device can play them, so you often encode more than one.

1Always high quality, always low quality, or adaptive: who stalls?

Adaptive bitrate on a bumpy network. The viewer moves through good Wi-Fi, a tunnel and a weak 4G area. Always playing the best quality stalls repeatedly whenever the bandwidth drops below the video's bitrate. Always playing the lowest quality never stalls but looks poor throughout. The adaptive player measures the last download and its buffer, chooses the highest rendition it can finish in time (more cautiously when the buffer is low), and gets close to the best of both: very few or no stalls with a much better average quality than the lowest setting. Real players (and Netflix's research on this) use more refined buffer-based algorithms, but the trade-off is the same.

python3 - <<'EOF'
import random
rng = random.Random(11)
LADDER = [235, 750, 1750, 4300]                       # kbps
SEG = 4                                                # seconds of video per segment
# a bandwidth trace: a phone moving between good Wi-Fi, a tunnel, and 4G (kbps), changing every segment time
bw = []
for phase, (lo, hi, n) in enumerate(((5000, 8000, 20), (300, 900, 10), (1500, 3500, 20), (200, 600, 8), (3000, 6000, 12))):
    bw += [rng.uniform(lo, hi) for _ in range(n)]
def play(chooser):
    buffer, t, stalls, stall_time, rates, last_tp = 8.0, 0.0, 0, 0.0, [], bw[0]
    for i, tp in enumerate(bw):
        rate = chooser(last_tp, buffer)
        download = rate * SEG / tp                                   # seconds to download this segment at the current bandwidth
        if download > buffer:                                        # the buffer runs dry while downloading: a stall
            stalls += 1; stall_time += download - buffer; buffer = 0.0
        else: buffer -= download
        buffer = min(buffer + SEG, 30.0); last_tp = tp; rates.append(rate)
    return stalls, stall_time, sum(rates) / len(rates)
fixed_high = lambda tp, buf: 4300
fixed_low = lambda tp, buf: 235
def adaptive(tp, buf):
    safe = tp * (0.6 if buf < 10 else 0.85)                          # be cautious when the buffer is low
    return max([r for r in LADDER if r <= safe] or [LADDER[0]])
print('70 segments of 4 s (%d s of video) over a bandwidth trace with a tunnel in it:' % (70 * SEG))
print('%-26s %7s %12s %15s' % ('strategy', 'stalls', 'stalled for', 'average quality'))
for name, f in (('always 1080p (4300 kbps)', fixed_high), ('always 240p (235 kbps)', fixed_low), ('adaptive', adaptive)):
    s, st, avg = play(f); print('%-26s %7d %10.1f s %11.0f kbps' % (name, s, st, avg))
EOF

6Scale, failure and trade-offsDeep dive

Processing falls behind

A burst of uploads fills the queue. Playback of existing videos is unaffected; only new videos wait. Add workers, prioritise short and popular uploads, and show a clear processing status.

A new release is hot

Millions ask for the same first segments in the same minute. Pre-warm the edges, and use an origin shield (one mid-tier cache that collapses many edge misses into one origin request).

A CDN region fails

Players retry another edge or another CDN named in the manifest. Keep the manifest short-lived so you can steer traffic.

Lost uploads

Resumable multipart upload plus a completion call that verifies size and hash. Never mark a video ready until every rendition exists.

Piracy and abuse

Use DRM (encrypted segments with licensed keys) for protected content, signed URLs that expire, and safety scanning in the pipeline before publishing.

3. How to say it

A model answer, about two minutes aloud

"Bandwidth is the dominant cost: a billion hours a day at a few megabits is on the order of an exabyte a day, so everything serves the CDN. Uploads go straight to an object store with resumable multipart upload and presigned URLs, and an event queues a processing job. The pipeline splits the source into chunks, transcodes them in parallel into a ladder of renditions and codecs, packages short segments and HLS or DASH manifests, generates thumbnails and runs safety checks, orchestrated as a workflow with retries and idempotent tasks.

Segments sit in an object store with metadata in a database. Viewers fetch the manifest and the player picks a rendition per segment from measured throughput and buffer, dropping quality before the buffer runs dry. CDN edges serve the segments, with popular titles pre-positioned and origin shielding to protect the store. Processing can lag without affecting playback of existing titles, and a CDN failure falls back to another. The trade-offs are encode and storage cost against quality options, segment length against latency, and codec efficiency against device support."

4. Likely follow-ups

Why short segments rather than one big file?

Adaptation per segment, fast start, seeking, cacheable small objects and resumable delivery.

How do you start playback quickly?

Start with a low rendition and a small first segment, prefetch, keep manifests small and served from the edge.

How would live streaming differ?

Segments are produced continuously by a packager, the manifest is updated, end-to-end delay is a few segments, and a CDN with low-latency modes distributes them.

Common slips

  • Serving one big file from the application servers.
  • No transcoding or bitrate ladder.
  • Ignoring that bandwidth, not compute, is the main cost.

8Remember thisRecap

The page in 6 lines

  • Delivery is the cost: about 1.4 exabytes a day in our estimate. Design for the CDN.
  • Upload straight to object storage with presigned URLs and resumable parts.
  • A queue and parallel workers turn one source into a ladder of renditions and segments.
  • A manifest lists the qualities and segments; the player adapts one segment at a time.
  • Adaptive playback stalls far less than fixed 1080p in the recorded run (2 stalls against 43).
  • Processing can lag without hurting playback; say how you pre-warm a hot release.

Check yourself

Why cut video into 4 second segments?

Each is a small cacheable request, the player can change quality between segments, and a failed piece is cheap to retry.

Why not stream through your own app servers?

Delivery is the dominant cost; a CDN serves most requests from the edge and keeps the origin nearly idle.

What does the manifest contain?

The list of renditions with their bitrates, and the addresses of each segment.
