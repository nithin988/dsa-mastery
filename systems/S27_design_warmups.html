Design a URL shortener: from requirements to numbers to a system

Must know
Situation

"Design a URL shortener" is the most common first system design question, and the most often answered badly: a box diagram with no numbers. This lesson builds the answer the way you should give it: requirements, estimates, code space, collisions, redirects, scaling and failure.

You will learn
  • Requirements, estimates and the read to write ratio
  • How many characters a code needs (and what base62 gives)
  • Collisions: random codes versus counters, measured
  • 301 versus 302 and what analytics needs
Mental model

Requirements. Functional: create a short code for a long URL, redirect from the code, optionally custom aliases, expiry and click analytics. Non-functional: redirects must be fast and highly available (they sit in every link that is shared), writes can be slower, links must not collide, and codes should not be guessable if URLs are private. Estimates: say 100 million new URLs a month is about 40 writes per second; with a read to write ratio of 100 to 1 that is about 4,000 redirects per second (peaks 5 to 10 times higher); at 500 bytes per record, five years is about 3 TB: modest. The shape is read-heavy and key-value: a cache in front of a simple store covers it.

The code. A code of length *n* in base62 (a-z, A-Z, 0-9) has 62ⁿ values: 6 characters give 56.8 billion, 7 give 3.5 trillion. Two ways to choose codes: random or hashed codes (hash the URL and truncate) can collide: by the birthday paradox, collisions appear much earlier than the size of the space suggests, so you must detect and retry; counter-based codes (a unique number from an ID generator, encoded in base62) never collide, are short, but are sequential and guessable unless you scramble them (a reversible permutation) and need a distributed counter (hand out ranges of numbers to each app server). 301 (permanent) redirects are cached by browsers, which saves load but hides later clicks from your analytics; 302 (temporary) hits you every time and lets you count and change the target. Click analytics should be asynchronous (a queue), never on the redirect path.

Everyday analogy: A coat-check: you hand in a coat (the long URL) and get a small numbered ticket (the code). The numbers must never be reused while the coat is there, handing out tickets must be quick, and finding a coat from its ticket must be instant, because everyone is waiting at the counter.

The picture
How it works, step by step
  1. Clarify and estimateWrites per second, redirects per second, retention, custom aliases, analytics: the numbers drive the design.
  2. Choose the code space and schemeLength from the capacity table; counters (no collisions) or random with retry.
  3. Define the API and data modelPOST creates, GET redirects; one row per code: code, long URL, created, expiry, owner.
  4. Make reads fast and availableA cache in front (hot codes), replicas, a CDN or edge for the redirect itself.
  5. Handle abuse, analytics and failuresRate limits, malware checks, async click counting, what happens when the ID generator is down.

chars = '0123456789abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyzABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ'

    s = ''
    for _ in range(width): n, r = divmod(n, 62); s += chars[r]

# 1. truncated hashes: the birthday paradox in action (5-character codes: 916 million possibilities, only 100,000 URLs)
N, W = 100_000, 5
seen, collisions = {}, 0
for i in range(N):
    url = 'https://example.com/page/%d' % i
    code = b62(int(hashlib.sha256(url.encode()).hexdigest(), 16), W)
    if code in seen: collisions += 1
    seen[code] = url
expected = N * N / (2 * 62 ** W)
print('hashing %d URLs into %d-character codes (%s possible): %d collisions (the birthday estimate predicts about %.1f)' % (N, W, '{:,}'.format(62 ** W), collisions, expected))
print('even a tiny fraction of the space in use produces collisions: you must detect and retry, or avoid hashing.')

db.execute('create table c(code text primary key)'); n = 0

Why "just hash the URL" is a trap. Hashing 100,000 URLs into five characters (916 million possible codes, so the space is 99.99 percent empty) still produces a handful of collisions, as the birthday formula predicts: n²/2m. A fuller code space needs constant retries: the database's primary key constraint is the safety net that makes retrying correct, because two requests can never insert the same code. A counter encoded in base62 has no collisions at all, which is why most real designs use one (often allocating ranges of numbers to each application server so no central bottleneck exists), scrambling the order if codes must not be guessable.

The redirect decision, in two responses. A 302 goes back to the shortener on every click, so every click can be counted and the destination can be changed later. A 301 carries Cache-Control: max-age=31536000: browsers and proxies remember it for a year, which removes load from your servers but means later clicks are invisible to your analytics and the link can never be repointed. Choose by the requirement: analytics or editable links need 302; pure, immutable shortening can use 301 (or a short max-age).

Interview in practice

"Design a URL shortener like bit.ly. Walk me through it."

The question: The canonical warm-up. A strong answer has structure and numbers: requirements, estimates, API, data model, architecture, deep dives, failure.

Pause for a minute and say how you would start, then compare with the walkthrough below.

1. Clarify first (what to ask, and why it matters)
  • Do we need custom aliases, expiry, analytics, private links? What scale? Assume: 100 million new URLs a month, 100:1 read to write, 5-year retention, custom aliases optional, click analytics wanted, redirects must be fast.
  • What availability do we need for redirects versus creation? Redirects: very high (99.99%); creation can be a little less strict.
2. Think it through (the reasoning, step by step)
  1. Estimate. About 40 writes/s and 4,000 reads/s (peak 40,000/s); 3 TB in five years; the working set of hot links is small and cacheable.
  2. API. POST /v1/urls {long_url, alias?, expires_at?} returns {short_url}; GET /{code} returns 302 Location; GET /v1/urls/{code}/stats. Authenticate creators, rate limit creation.
  3. Choose the code scheme. 7 base62 characters (3.5 trillion) from a counter: no collisions. Each app server leases a range of numbers (for example 1 million at a time) from a small coordination service so there is no per-request central call; scramble with a reversible permutation if codes must be unguessable. Custom aliases are checked for uniqueness with the primary key constraint.
  4. Data model. A key-value table: code (primary key) -> long_url, owner, created_at, expires_at. A sharded relational or wide-column/key-value store works; partition by code (hash).
  5. Read path. Redirect service checks the cache (Redis, LRU) first; on a miss reads the store and fills the cache; 302 response. Cache hit ratio above 95 percent means the store sees a few hundred reads a second. Add a CDN/edge for global latency if needed.
  6. Analytics. Publish a click event to a queue asynchronously (never block the redirect); a consumer aggregates counts by code and time bucket in an analytics store.
  7. Failures and abuse. Store replicated across zones; if the ID range service is down, servers continue with their leased range; malware and phishing checks on creation; rate limits; expiry sweeps; takedown process.
  8. Say the trade-offs. 301 versus 302; counters versus hashes; consistency (a new code must be readable immediately: read-your-writes for creators); cost of cache memory.
3. A strong answer, said out loud

"Let me start with numbers. At 100 million new URLs a month that is about 40 writes per second and, at 100 to 1, about 4,000 redirects per second with peaks ten times that, and roughly 3 terabytes over five years: small, read-heavy and key-value. The API is POST to create a short URL and GET on the code, which returns a 302 so I can count clicks and change targets.

For codes I use seven base62 characters from a counter: 3.5 trillion values and no collisions, whereas hashing and truncating collides early by the birthday paradox. Each app server leases a range of numbers from a small coordination service so there is no central call per request, and I scramble the order if codes must not be guessable. The data is one key-value row per code, sharded by hash of the code. Redirects check a Redis cache first, with a hit ratio above 95 percent, so the store sees a few hundred reads a second; clicks are published to a queue and aggregated asynchronously so analytics never slows the redirect. Everything is replicated across zones; if the range service is down, servers keep using their leased range. I would add rate limits, abuse checks, expiry and a takedown path."

4. Follow-ups you should expect
How would you prevent guessable or enumerable codes?

Apply a reversible permutation (a block cipher over the counter) before base62-encoding, or use random codes with collision retry; add rate limits on lookups.

What happens when the same long URL is submitted twice?

Either create a new code each time (simple, allows per-user analytics) or deduplicate by hash of the URL per user; state the choice.

How do you scale reads to 400,000 per second?

More cache replicas, consistent hashing across cache nodes, CDN or edge caching of the 302 with a short TTL, and replicas of the store.

Common slips
  • No numbers at all.
  • Hashing and truncating without discussing collisions.
  • Counting clicks synchronously on the redirect path.
Quick check

Answer in your head first, then open it.

How many base62 characters give about 3.5 trillion codes?

Seven (62^7).

Why do truncated hashes collide earlier than expected?

The birthday paradox: collisions appear when the number of items is about the square root of the space.

When would you choose 301 over 302?

When links are immutable and you do not need per-click analytics; 301 is cached by clients.

Traps to avoid (3)
  • Putting analytics writes on the redirect path.
  • A single ID generator that every request calls: a bottleneck and a single point of failure.
  • Forgetting that the cache hit ratio, not the database, determines how many reads the store sees.

Design a unique ID generator: Snowflake IDs and why index locality matters

Must know
Situation

Every row, order and message needs an ID, generated on many machines at once, without a central bottleneck, unique forever, and ideally sortable by time. Auto-increment breaks the moment you shard, and random UUIDs quietly slow your database down.

You will learn
  • Why auto-increment fails at scale
  • The Snowflake layout: time, machine, sequence
  • Clock problems and how generators cope
  • UUIDs versus time-ordered IDs, measured in a real B-tree
Mental model

A database auto-increment gives dense, ordered IDs but needs one writer: it does not survive sharding or multi-master. A UUID v4 is 128 random bits: unique with no coordination, but random, and as a primary key it scatters inserts across the B-tree, causing page splits, poor cache locality and larger indexes. A Snowflake-style ID packs a timestamp (milliseconds since a custom epoch, 41 bits, about 69 years), a machine id (10 bits, 1,024 machines) and a per-millisecond sequence (12 bits, 4,096 IDs per millisecond per machine) into 64 bits: unique without coordination (each machine owns its id), roughly time-ordered (so inserts append to the right edge of the index), compact, and decodable for debugging. ULIDs and UUID v7 apply the same idea to 128-bit formats.

The hard part is time. If the clock goes backwards (NTP step, VM pause) the generator may repeat a timestamp and duplicate IDs: the safe behaviour is to wait or refuse until the clock catches up, never to continue. Machine ids must be assigned uniquely (from a coordination service, or derived from a lease). If more than 4,096 IDs are needed in one millisecond the generator waits for the next one. Alternatives: ticket servers or range allocation (each server leases a block of numbers: simple, dense, no clocks), and a central sequence per entity when strict gap-free ordering is required (which needs a single leader). State what ordering you actually need: unique, sortable by time, or strictly sequential are three different requirements.

Everyday analogy: Instead of one clerk numbering every form (a bottleneck), each branch stamps forms with the date and time, its own branch number and a counter for forms in the same moment: any two forms in the world are distinguishable, and sorting by number sorts them roughly by time.

How it works, step by step
  1. Decide what "unique" must meanUnique only, sortable by time, or strictly sequential?
  2. Pack the fieldsTimestamp, machine id, sequence number into 64 bits.
  3. Give each machine its own idFrom a coordination service or lease: duplicates here break everything.
  4. Handle the clockWait on a backwards step; wait for the next millisecond when the sequence is exhausted.
  5. Check what it does to the indexTime-ordered IDs append; random IDs scatter.
A Snowflake generator, and what ID choice does to a B-tree 
cd ~/warm && python3 - <<'EOF'
EPOCH = 1_700_000_000_000            # custom epoch in ms
class Snowflake:
    def __init__(self, machine, clock):
        self.machine, self.clock, self.last, self.seq = machine, clock, -1, 0
    def next(self):
        now = self.clock()
        if now < self.last: raise RuntimeError('clock moved backwards by %d ms: refuse to issue IDs' % (self.last - now))
        if now == self.last:
            self.seq = (self.seq + 1) & 0xFFF
            if self.seq == 0:                                              # 4096 IDs used in this millisecond: wait for the next one
                while now <= self.last: now = self.clock() + 1
        else: self.seq = 0
        self.last = now
        return ((now - EPOCH) << 22) | (self.machine << 12) | self.seq
def decode(i): return {'ms_since_epoch': i >> 22, 'machine': (i >> 12) & 0x3FF, 'sequence': i & 0xFFF}
t = [1_700_000_500_000]
clock = lambda: t[0]
gens = [Snowflake(m, clock) for m in (1, 2, 3)]
ids = []
for step in range(4000):                                                   # three machines issue IDs; the clock ticks every 5 requests

    for g in gens: ids.append(g.next())
print('generated %d IDs on 3 machines: %d unique, strictly increasing within a machine: %s' % (len(ids), len(set(ids)), all(a < b for a, b in zip([i for i in ids[0::3]], [i for i in ids[0::3]][1:]))))
print('example ID %d decodes to %s' % (ids[10], decode(ids[10])))
print('sorted by ID is (nearly) sorted by time: first 3 decoded:', [decode(i)['ms_since_epoch'] for i in sorted(ids)[:3]])
print('capacity: %d IDs per millisecond per machine, %d machines, about %.0f years from the epoch' % (4096, 1024, (1 << 41) / (365.25 * 24 * 3600 * 1000)))
t[0] -= 5
try: gens[0].next()
except RuntimeError as e: print('clock went backwards:', e)
EOF

A complete Snowflake generator in thirty lines. Three machines issue 12,000 IDs with the clock advancing only every few requests, so many IDs share a millisecond: the sequence field disambiguates them, the machine field separates generators, and all 12,000 are unique with no coordination between machines. Decoding an ID recovers its timestamp, machine and sequence, handy when debugging. The last lines show the essential safety rule: if the clock moves backwards, the generator refuses to issue IDs rather than risk duplicating one.

Why ID choice is a performance decision. Inserting 300,000 rows into a B-tree whose cache is smaller than the table: time-ordered keys always land at the right edge, so the same few pages stay hot and pages fill completely. Random UUIDs land everywhere, forcing the database to touch and split pages all over the tree: the inserts are slower, and the file is larger because pages are left half empty. At larger scale (indexes bigger than memory) the gap becomes dramatic, with each insert causing a random disk read. This is the practical reason to prefer Snowflake, ULID or UUID v7 over UUID v4 for primary keys.

Design a key-value store: write-ahead log, memtable, recovery and partitioning

Must know
Situation

"Design a key-value store" asks for the core of every database in one question: how data survives a crash, how reads and writes stay fast, and how it spreads across machines. A toy you build and break yourself makes the answer concrete.

You will learn
  • The write path: WAL first, then memory
  • Crash recovery by log replay, demonstrated
  • Flushing, tombstones and compaction
  • Partitioning and replicating across nodes
Mental model

A single-node store durably accepts put(key, value) by first appending to a write-ahead log (WAL) on disk (sequential, fast), then updating an in-memory sorted structure (the memtable), then acknowledging. If the process crashes, memory is lost but the log is not: on restart the store replays the log to rebuild the memtable, so every acknowledged write survives. When the memtable is full it is flushed to an immutable sorted file (SSTable) and the log can be truncated. Reads check the memtable, then the files from newest to oldest (a Bloom filter per file avoids most useless lookups). A delete writes a tombstone (a marker), because old files are immutable; compaction merges files in the background, keeping the newest version of each key and dropping tombstones and obsolete values. This is the LSM design used by Cassandra, RocksDB and many others.

To scale beyond one node: partition keys across nodes by consistent hashing (a ring with virtual nodes, so adding a node moves only about 1/N of the keys), and replicate each key to N nodes (typically 3, the next nodes on the ring) in different failure domains. Choose the consistency per request with quorums (W + R > N for strong reads), handle a temporarily dead replica with hinted handoff and repair divergence with read repair and anti-entropy (Merkle trees), and detect failures with gossip. Clients (or a coordinator node) route each request to the right replicas. Each of these ideas appeared earlier in the course; a good answer assembles them and states the consistency and availability choice.

Everyday analogy: A clerk who writes every instruction in a ledger before touching the filing cabinet: if the building loses power mid-task, the ledger tells them exactly what to redo. Periodically the ledger's entries are filed into sorted folders, and a deleted record is marked rather than torn out until the folders are merged.

The picture
How it works, step by step
  1. Append to the logSequential write: durable once flushed to disk.
  2. Update the memtableA sorted in-memory map for fast reads and ordered flushing.
  3. Flush to sorted filesImmutable, so no in-place updates; deletes become tombstones.
  4. Compact in the backgroundMerge files, keep the newest version, drop tombstones.
  5. Partition and replicateConsistent hashing and N replicas across failure domains.

import json, os, glob
class KV:
    def __init__(self, d, limit=500):
        self.d, self.limit = d, limit; os.makedirs(d, exist_ok=True)
        self.mem = {}; self.segments = sorted(glob.glob(d + '/seg-*.json'))     # immutable sorted files, oldest first
        self.wal = d + '/wal.log'
        if os.path.exists(self.wal):                                            # RECOVERY: replay the log into a fresh memtable
            for line in open(self.wal):
                op = json.loads(line); self.mem[op['k']] = op['v']
        self.log = open(self.wal, 'a')
    def put(self, k, v):
        self.log.write(json.dumps({'k': k, 'v': v}) + '\n'); self.log.flush(); os.fsync(self.log.fileno())   # 1. durable first
        self.mem[k] = v                                                        # 2. then memory
        if len(self.mem) >= self.limit: self.flush()                            # 3. a full memtable becomes a sorted file
    def delete(self, k): self.put(k, None)                                      # a tombstone: None means deleted
    def flush(self):

import bisect, zlib
nodes = ['node-A', 'node-B', 'node-C', 'node-D', 'node-E']
ring = sorted((zlib.crc32(('%s#%d' % (n, v)).encode()), n) for n in nodes for v in range(64))

Design a pastebin: blobs, metadata, deduplication and expiry

Good to know
Situation

You will learn

Mental model

Split the data by what it is: metadata (id, owner, created, expires, size, content hash, visibility) is small, structured and queried: a database row. The content is large, immutable and read by key: it belongs in an object store (S3-like) or a blob table, not in the database row where it bloats pages and backups. Content-addressed storage names each blob by the hash of its content: identical pastes (very common: logs, templates, copied snippets) are stored once, many metadata rows point to the same blob, and integrity is checkable. The read path is a cache (hot pastes are read thousands of times) in front of the metadata and blob lookups.

Expiry is a real design point: scanning the whole table for expired rows is expensive. Do lazy deletion (a read of an expired paste returns 404 and removes it) plus a background sweep that deletes expired metadata in batches using an index on expires_at, and delete a blob only when no metadata row references it (reference counting or a periodic mark-and-sweep). Add limits and abuse controls: maximum size, rate limits per user and IP, content scanning for malware and secrets, unguessable ids for private pastes (the id is the capability), and a takedown mechanism. State the numbers: for example 1 million new pastes a day at 10 KB is 10 GB a day, 3.6 TB a year; reads at 10 to 1 are about 120 per second: modest.

Everyday analogy: A left-luggage office with a catalogue card for each ticket (metadata) and the actual bags on shelves (blobs). If two customers deposit identical suitcases, one suitcase can serve both tickets. Expired tickets are tidied away by a night shift, and a bag is only removed when no ticket points to it.

  6. Expiry. Lazy check on read; background sweep using an index on expires_at; delete blobs when unreferenced; keep deletes in small batches to avoid load spikes.
  7. Abuse and safety. Per-IP and per-account rate limits, size limits, malware and secret scanning, abuse reports and takedown, no execution of content, content-type sniffing off.
  8. Cost. Object storage with lifecycle rules (cold tier for old pastes), deduplication, compression; capacity is cheap, requests and egress are the costs.
3. A strong answer, said out loud

"At a million pastes a day that is about 12 writes per second and, at ten to one, about 120 reads per second, with roughly 10 gigabytes a day of content: a small system. I would split metadata from content: a table with the id, owner, content hash, size, expiry and visibility, and the bytes in an object store addressed by the hash of the content, so identical pastes are stored once. Ids are random base62 strings, which also make unlisted pastes unguessable.

Writes validate size, rate limit, hash, store the blob if new and insert the row; large uploads go straight to object storage with a pre-signed URL. Reads go through a cache and optionally a CDN: content is immutable, so caching needs no invalidation except on deletion or expiry. Expiry is lazy on read plus a background sweep over an index on the expiry time, deleting a blob only when nothing references it. I would add rate limits, size caps, scanning for malware and secrets, and a takedown path, and use storage lifecycle rules to move old pastes to a cold tier."

4. Follow-ups you should expect
How do you expire millions of pastes without scanning the table?

An index on expires_at lets the sweep read only expired rows in batches; also lazy expiry on read; or partition the table by expiry date and drop whole partitions.

Why is the id a capability for unlisted pastes?

Anyone with the link can read it, so the id must be long and random; do not use sequential ids.

How would you add editing or versions?

A new immutable blob per version with a version list in metadata: content addressing already supports it, and the cache stays valid.

Common slips
  • Storing large content in the database row.
  • A full table scan for expiry.
  • Sequential ids for private pastes.
Quick check

Answer in your head first, then open it.

What is content-addressed storage?

Storing each blob under the hash of its content, so identical content is stored once.

How do you expire many records cheaply?

Index the expiry time and delete in batches (or drop time partitions), plus lazy deletion on read.

Why separate metadata from blobs?

Small, queried rows suit a database; large immutable bytes suit an object store; mixing them bloats pages and backups.

Traps to avoid (3)
  • Deleting a blob when one paste expires although other pastes share it.
  • Sequential ids on private content.
  • No size and rate limits: the service becomes a free file host for abuse.

Design a distributed rate limiter: atomic token buckets in Redis

Must know
Situation

An API runs on forty servers. A customer is limited to 100 requests per second in total, not per server. The counters must be shared, and "read the count, then write the new count" will over-admit as soon as two servers do it at once. This is a concurrency problem as much as an algorithm.

You will learn
  • Why check-then-update races
  • A token bucket as a Lua script in Redis (atomic)
  • Choosing keys, limits and failure behaviour
  • Local approximation versus central accuracy
Mental model

A shared limiter keeps a counter per customer in a fast central store (Redis). The naive implementation, GET the tokens, decide, SET the new value, has a race: two servers read the same value, both admit, both write: the limit is exceeded under concurrency, exactly when it matters. The fix is to make the read-modify-write atomic: a Lua script executed by Redis runs without interleaving, so it can read the bucket, refill it by the elapsed time, take a token and write it back as one operation; or use atomic commands (INCR with expiry for fixed windows). A token bucket needs only two numbers per key (tokens and last refill time), so memory is tiny and the script is fast (tens of microseconds).

Design decisions: the key (API key, user, IP, plus endpoint class), the limits and tiers, the response (429 with Retry-After and X-RateLimit-* headers), where to enforce (gateway, before the work), and failure behaviour when Redis is down (usually fail open with a conservative local limit, because a limiter outage should not become an API outage; fail closed only for abuse-critical endpoints). For very high rates, avoid one hot key and one round trip per request with local token buckets that borrow batches of tokens from the central one (accuracy traded for latency), or shard the limit across servers. Expire idle keys so memory does not grow.

Everyday analogy: A single till with a single cashier: if two shop assistants each check the float in the drawer and both hand out the last note, the drawer is overdrawn. The fix is one person who checks and hands out in one motion, which is what the Lua script is.

How it works, step by step
  1. Choose the key and the limitPer customer and endpoint class, with a burst and a refill rate.
  2. Run the check as one atomic scriptRefill by elapsed time, take a token if available, store, return allowed or retry-after.
  3. Answer fast and clearly200 or 429 with Retry-After; never queue unbounded.
  4. Decide what happens if the store is downFail open with a local fallback, and alert.
  5. Scale the hot pathLocal buckets that borrow tokens in batches for very high rates.

# A minimal Redis client (the Redis wire protocol is simple text), so the experiments need nothing but python3.
import socket
class R:
    def __init__(self, port=6396):
        self.s = socket.create_connection(('127.0.0.1', port)); self.f = self.s.makefile('rb')
    def cmd(self, *a):
        out = b'*%d\r\n' % len(a)
        for x in a:
            x = str(x).encode(); out += b'$%d\r\n%s\r\n' % (len(x), x)
        self.s.sendall(out); return self._read()
    def _read(self):
        line = self.f.readline().rstrip(b'\r\n'); t, val = line[:1], line[1:]
        if t == b'+': return val.decode()
        if t == b'-': raise Exception(val.decode())
        if t == b':': return int(val)
        if t == b'$':
            n = int(val)
            return None if n < 0 else self.f.read(n + 2)[:-2].decode()
        if t == b'*': return [self._read() for _ in range(int(val))]
EOF

import sys, threading, time

LUA = """
local tokens = tonumber(redis.call('HGET', KEYS[1], 't'))
local last = tonumber(redis.call('HGET', KEYS[1], 'ts'))
local cap, rate, now = tonumber(ARGV[1]), tonumber(ARGV[2]), tonumber(ARGV[3])
if tokens == nil then tokens = cap; last = now end
tokens = math.min(cap, tokens + (now - last) * rate)
local allowed = 0
if tokens >= 1 then tokens = tokens - 1; allowed = 1 end
redis.call('HSET', KEYS[1], 't', tokens, 'ts', now)
redis.call('PEXPIRE', KEYS[1], 60000)
return allowed
"""
def naive(r):
    t = r.cmd('GET', 'naive')
    if t is not None and int(t) > 0:
        time.sleep(NAIVE_SLEEP); r.cmd('SET', 'naive', int(t) - 1); return 1                 # read, decide, then write: NOT atomic
    return 0
def atomic(r):
    return r.cmd('EVAL', LUA, 1, 'bucket', LIMIT, 0, 1000)                                    # refill rate 0: only the initial 10 tokens exist
def run(fn, threads=30, calls=2):
    allowed = [0]; lock = threading.Lock()
    def work():
        r = R()
        for _ in range(calls):
            a = fn(r)
            with lock: allowed[0] += a
    ts = [threading.Thread(target=work) for _ in range(threads)]; [t.start() for t in ts]; [t.join() for t in ts]
    return allowed[0]
r = R(); r.cmd('FLUSHALL'); r.cmd('SET', 'naive', LIMIT)
print('limit: %d requests. 30 concurrent clients each try twice (60 attempts).' % LIMIT)
print('naive GET, decide, SET:        %2d requests admitted' % run(naive))
print('atomic Lua token bucket:       %2d requests admitted' % run(atomic))

The Lua script executes inside Redis as one uninterrupted operation, so the check and the update cannot interleave: exactly ten are admitted, however many clients arrive. Interview in practice

"Design a rate limiter that works across a fleet of API servers and handles 1 million requests per second."

The question: The distributed version of the rate limiter question: the algorithm is easy, the distribution and the failure modes are the point.

Pause for a minute and say how you would start, then compare with the walkthrough below.

1. Clarify first (what to ask, and why it matters)
  • What do we limit by, how accurate must it be, and what happens at the limit: reject, queue or degrade? Assume per API key and endpoint class, small inaccuracy acceptable, reject with 429.
2. Think it through (the reasoning, step by step)
  1. Algorithm. Token bucket: bursts allowed, average enforced; two numbers per key.
  2. Centralised counters. A Redis cluster sharded by key; one atomic Lua script per check (refill, take, write); about 100,000 checks per second per Redis node, so 1 million per second needs ten or more shards, and consistent hashing by key.
  3. Reduce the central load. Local token buckets in each gateway that borrow a batch of tokens (for example 10 at a time) from the central bucket: far fewer round trips; the inaccuracy is bounded by the batch size times the number of gateways.
  4. Hot keys. One huge customer is one hot key: split their limit into sub-buckets per gateway or shard the bucket (divide the limit by N and spread).
  5. Failure. Redis down or slow: fail open with a conservative local limit, alert; time-box the Redis call (a few milliseconds) so the limiter never adds more latency than a rejection would save.
  6. API behaviour. 429 with Retry-After and X-RateLimit headers; limits visible to customers; tiers; allow-lists; separate limits for expensive endpoints.
  7. Operate. Metrics: allowed and rejected per key, limiter latency, Redis memory; configuration hot-reloadable; test with load and failure injection.
3. A strong answer, said out loud

"I would use a token bucket per customer and endpoint class: it allows bursts and enforces an average, and needs only two numbers per key. The counters live in a sharded Redis, and every check is one Lua script that refills, takes a token and writes back atomically, because a read-then-write check races under concurrency and over-admits. A Redis node handles on the order of a hundred thousand such checks a second, so a million per second needs a dozen shards by consistent hashing of the key.

To cut the central load, each gateway keeps a local bucket that borrows a small batch of tokens from the central one, trading a bounded inaccuracy for far fewer round trips, and a hot customer's bucket is split across sub-buckets. If Redis is slow or down I fail open with a conservative local limit and an alert, with a few millisecond timeout so the limiter never costs more than it saves. Rejected calls get 429 with Retry-After and rate-limit headers, and I monitor allowed and rejected counts per key and limiter latency."

4. Follow-ups you should expect
Why a Lua script and not a transaction with WATCH?

The script is simpler, faster and cannot be interleaved; optimistic transactions with WATCH retry under contention, which is exactly when many clients hit one hot key.

Fixed window, sliding window or token bucket?

Fixed window is cheapest but allows 2x bursts at the boundary; sliding window is accurate but costs more memory; token bucket gives bursts plus an average and is the usual choice.

How accurate is the local-bucket approach?

Error is bounded by the batch size times the number of gateways; choose the batch so the worst case overshoot is acceptable.

Common slips
  • Describing GET then SET as if it were safe.
  • A single Redis key as a global bottleneck at a million requests per second.
  • Failing closed when the limiter store is unavailable.
Quick check

Answer in your head first, then open it.

Why does GET-then-SET over-admit under concurrency?

Several clients read the same value before any writes it back, so all are admitted.

How does a Lua script in Redis help?

It runs atomically, so refill, take and write cannot interleave with other clients.

What should happen when the limiter's store is down?

Usually fail open with a conservative local limit and an alert.

Traps to avoid (3)
  • Per-server counters with no sharing: the real limit is N times higher.
  • One hot key for the busiest customer: use sub-buckets.
  • A slow Redis call on the request path with no timeout.

Must know
Situation

Mental model

A design interview evaluates how you think: clarifying an ambiguous problem, choosing what matters, estimating, designing at the right level, reasoning about trade-offs and failure, and communicating while collaborating. Interview corner

Warm-up designs are judged on structure and numbers: clarify, estimate, choose with reasons, go deep where it is hard, and name failures and trade-offs. Practise each scenario aloud against the clock; the written answers are models, not scripts.

Design a URL shortener.
Shape of a strong answer
  • Requirements and estimates: 100M new URLs a month is ~40 writes/s, ~4,000 reads/s, ~3 TB over five years.
  • 7 base62 characters from a counter with leased ranges (no collisions) versus hashes (birthday collisions, retry); key-value store sharded by code; Redis cache; 302 and async click analytics.
  • Failure: replicated store, leased ranges survive the ID service outage; abuse checks, rate limits, expiry.
Follow-ups to expect
  • Guessable codes?
  • 301 or 302?
Common mistakes
  • No numbers.
  • Hashing without collision handling.
  • Synchronous analytics.

Company angle: Reported by candidates as the most common first design question at large companies; not official.

Design a unique ID generator for 100,000 IDs per second.
Shape of a strong answer
  • Clarify unique versus sortable versus sequential; reject auto-increment (single writer) and UUID v4 (random, 128 bits, bad locality).
  • Snowflake: 41-bit time, 10-bit machine id, 12-bit sequence: 4,096 per ms per machine; machine ids leased; clock handling (wait or refuse); sequence overflow waits.
  • Roughly ordered only across machines: use per-entity sequences for strict order.
Follow-ups to expect
  • Duplicate machine ids?
  • Gap-free numbers?
Common mistakes
  • Using timestamps as a strict global order.
Design a key-value store.
Shape of a strong answer
  • Single node: WAL, memtable, flush to sorted files, tombstones, compaction, Bloom filters, recovery by log replay.
  • Distributed: consistent hashing with virtual nodes, N=3 replicas across zones, tunable quorums, version vectors, read repair, hinted handoff, gossip, Merkle repair.
  • Trade-offs: AP by default, no multi-key transactions, ranges need ordered partitioning.
Follow-ups to expect
  • How does it survive a crash?
  • Why tombstones?
Common mistakes
  • Skipping durability.
  • Hash mod N with no rebalancing plan.

Company angle: Amazon-style: expect follow-ups on Dynamo concepts such as hinted handoff and quorums; reported by candidates, not official.

Design Pastebin with a million new pastes a day.
Shape of a strong answer
  • ~12 writes/s, ~120 reads/s, ~10 GB/day: small.
  • Metadata in a database, content in an object store addressed by hash (dedupe), random ids, cache, optional CDN.
  • Expiry: lazy plus indexed batch sweep; delete blobs when unreferenced; limits, scanning, takedown; lifecycle to cold storage.
Follow-ups to expect
  • Expiring millions of pastes?
  • Versions?
Common mistakes
  • Blobs in database rows.
  • Full scans for expiry.
Design a distributed rate limiter for a fleet doing a million requests per second.
Shape of a strong answer
  • Token bucket per key; atomic Lua script in sharded Redis (GET/SET races); consistent hashing of keys.
  • Local buckets borrowing token batches, sub-buckets for hot keys; fail open with local fallback and a timeout.
  • 429 with Retry-After and headers; metrics per key; tiers.
Follow-ups to expect
  • Why Lua rather than WATCH?
  • How accurate is local borrowing?
Common mistakes
  • Check-then-update races.
  • Single hot key.

Company angle: Google-style: candidates commonly report follow-ups on accuracy versus latency; reported by candidates, not official.

How do you structure and pace a 45-minute design interview?
Shape of a strong answer
  • State the plan: requirements, estimates, API and data, high-level design, deep dives and failure, wrap-up.
  • Numbers that justify choices, trade-offs stated, depth where it is hard, failure thinking, adaptability.
  • Avoid: jumping to solutions, technologies without reasons, equal time on every box, ignoring failures, being defensive.
Follow-ups to expect
  • What if you run out of time?
  • What if the interviewer disagrees?
Common mistakes
  • No requirements or numbers.

Self-test

Answer in your head first, then open the question.

How many base62 characters give about 3.5 trillion codes?

Seven.

Why do hashed short codes collide early?

The birthday paradox: collisions appear around the square root of the space.

301 versus 302 for a shortener?

301 is cached permanently (no analytics, fixed target); 302 is temporary (counted, editable).

What are the parts of a Snowflake ID?

41-bit timestamp, 10-bit machine id, 12-bit sequence.

What must a generator do if the clock goes backwards?

Wait or refuse to issue IDs.

Why are random UUIDs poor primary keys?

They scatter inserts across the B-tree.

Why write to the WAL before the memtable?

So acknowledged writes survive a crash.

What is a tombstone?

A marker for a delete in an immutable-file store, removed later by compaction.

What is content-addressed storage?

Blobs named by the hash of their content: identical content is stored once.

How do you expire many records cheaply?

Index the expiry time, batch deletes, and expire lazily on read.

Why does GET-then-SET over-admit?

Concurrent clients read the same value before any writes it back.

What is the usual limiter failure mode?

Fail open with a local fallback and an alert.

How long is 100 million requests per day per second?

About 1,200 requests per second.
