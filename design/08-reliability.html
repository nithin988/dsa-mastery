Chapter 8 of 9The ShopKart story

Keep it up

How to make a system that keeps working while parts of it break, parts of it are being changed, and far too many people show up at once.

about 18 minutes4 steps3 real experiments3 interview questions

1A bad release at 3 p.m. and a dead server at 3 a.m.The story

ShopKart has grown up. After the cache from chapter 3 and the retries from chapter 7, the shop is fast and mostly correct. But on a Tuesday at 3 p.m. the team ships a redesigned checkout to all customers at once. Twenty minutes later, support is flooded: some payments fail, and nobody can say why.

They roll back by hand and lose about an hour. Three nights later, at 3 a.m., the one machine running the database dies. The pager wakes the only engineer on call. Restoring service takes almost an hour of lost orders, plus the cost of the shop being dark in the middle of a sale.

Neither day was caused by a clever attack or a rare bug. One change reached everyone before anyone knew whether it was safe, and one machine could take down the business. Reliability is designing so that normal failures and normal mistakes stay small.

2The idea: assume it will fail, and make failure smallThe idea

Reliability means the system keeps doing its job even when parts fail. You get it by removing single points of failure, by changing things a little at a time, and by being able to see what is wrong and undo it quickly.

Every extra nine costs more. Two independent copies of a 99.9% service give 99.9999% on paper, but only if the copies fail separately: two copies in one zone die together when the zone does, and the real figure falls back to about 99.9%. So the design question is always: what fails together, and what happens next?

3How it works, step by stepHow it works

  1. Count the nines, then spread the blast radius

    Availability is the share of time you work: 99.9% allows 43.2 minutes of downtime in a 30-day month, 99.99% allows only 4.3. A failure domain is the set of things that die together (one machine, one rack, one zone), so keep copies in different domains.

  2. Restart, fail over, degrade

    A supervisor restarts a crashed process after a short wait that grows each time. A load balancer fails over by skipping a dead server and retrying on another. When an optional part such as recommendations is down, degrade gracefully: show the page without it.

  3. Release to a few, with a switch to undo

    A canary release sends a small share (say 5%) of traffic to the new version and compares its errors with the old one. If it is worse, roll back automatically. A feature flag hides new code behind a switch, so you turn it on for some users and off in seconds without a redeploy.

  4. Measure, so you notice before customers do

    Metrics are numbers over time (request rate, error ratio, latency percentiles). Logs are the events. A trace follows one request id across services. Alert on the slowest 1% (p99), not the average, which hides the pain.

4See it for realReal experiments

Three experiments. First: the arithmetic of the nines, including the trap of copies that fail together. Then: a canary that decides to roll back by itself. Then: what an overloaded service does with and without load shedding.

1The nines, and copies that fail together

The arithmetic that should precede any "we need five nines" conversation. 99.99 percent is 52 minutes a year, and 99.999 percent barely 5 minutes, less than one slow deployment. In series availabilities multiply (more components, lower availability); in parallel the unavailabilities multiply (two 99.9 percent copies reach 99.9999 percent). The composite example shows the lesson: once the application tier is duplicated, the single database dominates the downtime; adding a second copy removes most of it. The last lines show the catch: two copies in the same failure domain do not multiply anything, because they fail together.

2A canary that rolls itself back

A canary doing its job. The new version has a bug that fails 30 percent of requests, the stable version about half a percent. At the first step only 5 percent of traffic reaches the canary, so only about one request in twenty hits an error, and the decision rule (canary error rate above 5 percent) fires within the first measurement window: the traffic is routed back to the stable version automatically. Compare this with a big-bang release that would have given 30 percent errors to every user until a human noticed. The rule needs real metrics, a large enough sample and a rollback that is one configuration change.

3Overload: shed load or collapse

Congestion collapse in numbers. Up to the server's capacity (100 per second) both versions serve everything. Beyond it, the unprotected server keeps accepting requests, its queue grows without limit, every request waits longer than the client's one-second timeout, and the server spends all its effort on answers nobody is waiting for: useful throughput falls towards zero while the machine is 100 percent busy. The server with a bounded queue rejects the excess immediately and keeps delivering about its full capacity of useful answers, whatever the offered load. Rejecting some requests quickly is what allows the rest to succeed.

python3 - <<'EOF'
# Overload in virtual time: a server can finish 100 requests per second. Clients give up after 1 second and RETRY once.
# "goodput" = requests answered before the client gave up.
def simulate(offered_rate, shed, seconds=30, capacity=100, timeout=1.0, max_queue=50):
    queue = []                                    # arrival times of waiting requests (FIFO)
    good = bad = shed_count = 0
    carry = 0.0; served_budget = 0.0
    for tick in range(seconds * 100):            # 10 ms ticks
        now = tick / 100
        arrivals = offered_rate / 100
        n = int(arrivals + carry); carry = arrivals + carry - n
        for _ in range(n):
            if shed and len(queue) >= max_queue: shed_count += 1; continue        # reject early: cheap
            queue.append(now)
        served_budget += capacity / 100
        while served_budget >= 1 and queue:
            served_budget -= 1; arrived = queue.pop(0)                             # the server works on the oldest request first
            if now - arrived <= timeout: good += 1
            else: bad += 1                                                         # finished too late: the client already left; work wasted
    return good / seconds, bad / seconds, shed_count / seconds
print('capacity: 100 requests/s.  client timeout: 1 s.')
print('offered   WITHOUT shedding              WITH shedding (queue limit 50)')
print('load/s    useful/s  wasted/s            useful/s  wasted/s  rejected/s')
for rate in (50, 90, 100, 150, 300):
    g1, b1, _ = simulate(rate, False); g2, b2, r2 = simulate(rate, True)
    print('%6d    %7.0f  %7.0f             %7.0f  %7.0f  %9.0f' % (rate, g1, b1, g2, b2, r2))
EOF

5How it breaks in productionFailure modes

Counting copies that are not independent

Two servers behind one load balancer, in one zone, on one power feed, do not multiply availability. Fix: put copies in different failure domains, and list what they share (zone, database, config, deploy).

A weak link in the chain

Components in a chain multiply: three parts at 99.9% each give 99.7%. In the recorded example a single database at 99.95% was the weakest link, costing 316 minutes a year; a second copy cut that to 53 minutes.

Ship to everyone at once

A bug that reaches every user is an outage. Fix: canary at 5%, compare errors with the stable version, roll back automatically, and put risky changes behind a feature flag with a kill switch.

A dependency that drags you down

An optional service (recommendations) fails and the whole page fails with it. Fix: timeouts, fallbacks, and a stale or empty answer. In the recorded run the critical design served 713 of 1000 pages; the graceful one served all 1000.

Backups nobody has restored

A backup that was never tested may be broken and you find out in the disaster. Fix: restore automatically on a schedule, and measure restore time, because that is your real recovery time.

Overload turns into collapse

When load exceeds capacity, queues grow, clients time out, and the work done is wasted. Fix: reject early with a clear error (load shedding), keep queues short, and let clients back off.

7In the interviewTop-company questions

Say your answer aloud first, then compare with the model answer. These are the shapes of question candidates report, not official lists.

A common reliability prompt: reported by candidates at large tech companies, not official.

"The business wants 99.99% availability for checkout. How do you design for it, and what does it cost?"

An availability design question. They want arithmetic, failure domains, dependencies and honesty about cost.

Ask first

  • Is 99.99% measured at the user, per month, and what counts as down? What does an hour of downtime cost? Measure at the user's edge; define success with an SLI; compare the cost of downtime with the cost of the extra nines.

How to think about it

  1. Do the arithmetic.99.99 percent is about 4 minutes a month, 52 a year: shorter than most deployments and most manual recoveries, so failover and deployment must be automated.
  2. Budget the dependencies.List every dependency in the request path with its real availability (DNS, CDN, payment provider, database, cloud region). If a required dependency offers 99.9 percent, you cannot promise 99.99 without making it optional or redundant.
  3. Remove single points of failure across domains.Load balancers, app servers, databases and caches in at least two zones, N+1 capacity; multi-region if the region's own availability is not enough.
  4. Make failures cheap.Graceful degradation: checkout works without recommendations; fallbacks and cached data; timeouts, retries with backoff and circuit breakers; asynchronous steps through queues.
  5. Make changes safe.Most outages follow changes: canary releases, feature flags, automatic rollback, small batches, backward-compatible database migrations.
  6. Make recovery fast and tested.Automated failover, runbooks, game days, tested backups; detection in under a minute.
  7. State the cost.Roughly doubled infrastructure, more engineering and operational discipline per nine; propose the target the business will pay for and an error budget policy.

How to say it

A model answer, about two minutes aloud

"First the arithmetic: 99.99 percent is about four minutes of downtime a month, shorter than most deployments or manual recoveries, so failover and rollout must be automated. Then I list every dependency in the checkout path with its real availability, because if a required one offers 99.9 percent I either make it optional with a fallback or add redundancy; I cannot promise more nines than my dependencies have.

Then I remove single points of failure across failure domains: balancers, app servers, databases and caches in at least two zones with spare capacity, and a second region if the region's own availability is not enough. I make failures cheap with graceful degradation, timeouts, retries with backoff, circuit breakers and queues; make changes safe with canaries, flags and automatic rollback; and make recovery fast and tested with automated failover and game days. And I would state the cost, roughly twice the infrastructure plus engineering discipline, and agree an error budget so the business chooses the target knowingly."

Likely follow-ups

What is an error budget?

The allowed unreliability implied by the SLO (for example 0.01 percent): spend it on releases and experiments, and slow down when it is exhausted (the reliability practice module covers this).

Why are two servers in one zone not enough?

They share failure domains (power, network, zone control plane), so the independence assumption in the arithmetic is false.

When is multi-region worth it?

When the cost of regional outages exceeds the cost and complexity of running active-active or fast-failover across regions, or when latency or data residency requires it.

Common slips

  • Promising nines without listing dependencies.
  • Redundancy inside one failure domain.
  • Manual failover in a four-nines design.
A frequent operations question for senior roles: reported by candidates, not official.

"How would you deploy changes to a system serving millions of users with no downtime and the ability to undo mistakes?"

The standard release-engineering question. They want strategy, automation, data compatibility and what you watch.

Ask first

  • How often do we deploy, how fast can we detect bad releases, and does the change touch the database? Frequent small releases need automation; database changes need the expand-contract discipline.

How to think about it

  1. Small, frequent, automated.Trunk-based development, CI with tests, one pipeline to production, small changes that are easy to review and revert.
  2. Progressive delivery.Canary release: 1 percent, 5, 25, 100, with automated comparison of error rate, latency and business metrics against the baseline; automatic rollback on regression; bake time between steps.
  3. Separate deploy from release.Feature flags to turn features on gradually and off instantly, per user cohort or region.
  4. Compatible data changes.Expand, migrate, contract: add nullable columns or new tables first, deploy code that writes both, backfill, switch reads, remove the old later; never couple a destructive migration to a deploy.
  5. Zero downtime mechanics.Rolling or blue-green behind a load balancer with draining and readiness checks; stateless instances; connection draining; preStop hooks.
  6. Guard rails.Deployment freezes around peak events, change calendars, automatic halt when error budget is burned, cell-based or region-by-region rollout so a bad change cannot hit everywhere at once.
  7. Observe and learn.Dashboards by version, deploy markers on graphs, post-incident reviews feeding back into the pipeline.

How to say it

A model answer, about two minutes aloud

"I would make changes small, frequent and automated: trunk-based development, a single pipeline with tests, and progressive delivery. Each release goes to a canary first, 1 percent then 5, 25 and 100, with an automated comparison of error rate, latency and business metrics against the stable version and an automatic rollback when it regresses, with bake time between steps. Risky features ship behind flags so they can be turned on gradually and off instantly without a redeploy.

Database changes follow expand, migrate, contract so old and new code both work with the schema at every step, and destructive changes never ride along with a deploy. For zero downtime I use rolling or blue-green behind a balancer with readiness checks and connection draining, and I roll out region by region or cell by cell so a bad change cannot hit everyone. Deploy markers on the dashboards and post-incident reviews feed back into the pipeline."

Likely follow-ups

What is a cell-based architecture?

Dividing the system into independent cells, each serving a slice of customers, so a bad change or failure affects one cell, and rollouts proceed cell by cell.

How long should a canary run?

Long enough to collect a statistically meaningful sample and to cover cyclical behaviour (for example a few minutes at high traffic, longer at low traffic), with automatic checks.

How do you roll back a database change?

You usually do not: design changes to be backward compatible so the code can roll back while the schema stays; keep destructive steps separate and delayed.

Common slips

  • Big-bang releases.
  • Rollback that depends on a human noticing.
  • Destructive migrations tied to the deploy.
A common closing question about disasters and traffic spikes: reported by candidates, not official.

"Design disaster recovery for a payments database, and explain what happens when traffic doubles unexpectedly."

A two-part reliability question: recovery from loss and survival under overload. They want numbers and mechanisms.

Ask first

  • What are the RPO and RTO the business requires, and what is the data volume? Payments usually need RPO near zero and an RTO of minutes; that dictates synchronous replication and a hot standby.

How to think about it

  1. Define RPO and RTO.Payments: RPO of seconds or zero, RTO of minutes. Backups alone (RPO hours, RTO hours) are not enough.
  2. Choose the architecture.Synchronous replica in another zone (zero loss for a zone failure), asynchronous replica in another region (seconds of loss, survives a region), automated failover with fencing, continuous WAL archiving for point-in-time recovery.
  3. Backups.Regular base backups plus archived WAL to another account and region, some immutable or offline copies, encryption with keys held separately; protect against the compromised-credentials and operator-error scenarios.
  4. Test it.Automated restore tests with checksums and row counts, timed; quarterly failover and region-evacuation drills; measure the real RPO and RTO and report them.
  5. Overload part.Autoscaling takes minutes and databases do not scale instantly; so protect with rate limits per client, bounded queues and load shedding at the edge (503/429 with Retry-After), priority for critical operations (payments over reporting), timeouts and deadlines propagated, client backoff with retry budgets, and a degraded mode that disables expensive optional features.
  6. Avoid metastable failure.Do not let retries amplify: cap retries, circuit breakers; after an incident, ramp traffic back gradually.
  7. Operations.Runbooks, dashboards for saturation and queue depth, capacity reviews, load tests at 2x peak, game days.

How to say it

A model answer, about two minutes aloud

"First the objectives: for payments I would want an RPO of seconds or zero and an RTO of minutes, which backups alone cannot give. So: a synchronous replica in another zone, an asynchronous replica in another region, automated failover with fencing, and continuous archiving of the write-ahead log for point-in-time recovery, plus base backups copied to another account and region with some immutable copies and separate encryption keys. And I would restore them automatically and time it, and rehearse failover and region evacuation regularly, because the measured RTO is the only one that counts.

When traffic doubles unexpectedly, autoscaling takes minutes and a database cannot scale instantly, so the system must protect itself: rate limits per client, bounded queues, and load shedding at the edge with 503 and Retry-After, priority for payments over reporting, timeouts and deadlines propagated downstream, client backoff with retry budgets and circuit breakers so retries do not create a metastable failure, and a degraded mode that switches off expensive optional features. I would load test at twice the peak and hold game days."

Likely follow-ups

What is point-in-time recovery?

Restoring a base backup and replaying archived write-ahead log up to a chosen moment (such as just before a bad command), giving an RPO of seconds.

Why is your restore time longer than you think?

Restore must copy the data, rebuild indexes, replay logs and verify, often sequentially and over a network: measure it with production-sized data.

What is metastable failure?

A state where a feedback loop (typically retries) keeps the system overloaded after the original trigger is gone, so it stays down until load is reduced.

Common slips

  • Backups never restored.
  • Autoscaling as the only answer to overload.
  • Backups stored with the same credentials as production.

8Remember thisRecap

The page in 6 lines

  • Availability is time: 99.9% is 43.2 minutes a month, 99.99% is 4.3 minutes.
  • Chains multiply and lower availability; independent copies raise it. Shared failure domains break the maths.
  • Supervisors restart, load balancers fail over, and optional features degrade instead of failing the page.
  • Release to 5% first, compare errors, roll back automatically, and keep a flag as a kill switch.
  • Watch p99 and follow a request id across services; the average hides the pain.
  • Restore your backups on purpose, and shed load before overload collapses you.

Check yourself

What does 99.99% allow per month?

About 4.3 minutes of downtime in a 30-day month.

Why is a canary better than testing alone?

It exercises the new version on real traffic at small scale, with an automatic decision to roll back if errors rise.

What is load shedding?

Rejecting some requests early so the rest are served well, instead of queueing everything until all of it times out.
