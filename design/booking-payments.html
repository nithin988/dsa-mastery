Design booking and payments

Two promises that are easy to say and hard to keep under load: never sell the same seat twice, never charge the same card twice.

about 35 minutes3 real experimentsThe usual correctness focused design question

1Two tickets for seat 14CThe question

ShopKart sells 500 tickets for a launch event. The booking code reads the seat, sees it is free, then marks it sold. At noon, two customers click within the same few milliseconds. Both read free, both write sold, and both receive a confirmation. At the door there is one seat and two angry people.

A week later a different bug shows up. The payment provider is slow, the app times out and retries, and one customer is charged twice. The interviewer's question is the same one the team now has to answer: how do you design ticket booking so neither mistake can happen, even when a million people arrive at once?

The interviewer says: Design a ticket booking system like Ticketmaster. How do you avoid double booking and handle payment failures?

What they are looking for: The booking and payments capstone. They want atomic seat allocation, holds, payment orchestration and failure handling.

2What to ask before drawing anythingRequirements

Two minutes of questions is what separates a design from a guess. Write the answers on the board.

  • Peak demand (a famous concert), seat maps or general admission, hold time, payment providers, refunds? Assume 50,000 seats on sale at once with 1 million waiting, seat maps, 10 minute holds, an external payment provider.

Functional: browse events and seat maps, hold seats while paying, pay, receive a ticket, refund. Non-functional: a seat is never sold twice, a customer is never charged twice, holds expire on their own, and the system survives a flash sale. Money correctness is more important than speed.

3The numbers that shape the designEstimates

QuantityEstimate and what it tells you
The flash sale50,000 seats go on sale while 1 million people wait (the scenario's assumption). That is 20 buyers per seat, all arriving in the first minutes.
Reads versus writesBrowsing the event and seat map is cacheable and can be a little stale. Writes concentrate on a few thousand hot rows, the seats. Read load is not the problem; contention is.
HoldsA hold lasts 10 minutes. If 50,000 seats are held at once and a buyer needs about 2 minutes to pay, most holds are released early or expire. Holds must clean themselves up.
Payment callsIf a tenth of the million reach payment, that is 100,000 payment calls in a few minutes. The provider is slow and sometimes unavailable, so every payment must survive a timeout.
Ledger arithmeticIn the recorded run, one 4,999 cent order produces three balanced entries: -4,999, +4,900 and +99. They sum to zero, which is the check that no money appeared or vanished.

4A first design that worksArchitecture

  1. Let the database decide the seat

    Do not check in the application and then update. Use one statement: set the seat to held where its status is still available. The database runs it atomically, so exactly one buyer changes the row, and the other sees zero rows changed.

  2. Hold the seat with an expiry

    The same update also writes hold_until, ten minutes ahead. The buyer now has time to pay. If they leave, the next reader or a sweeper job sees the expired hold and frees the seat. No manual clean up is needed.

  3. Pay with an idempotency key

    Each payment attempt carries a unique key. The first request charges the card. A retry with the same key does not charge again; it replays the first answer. This turns the dangerous unknown timeout into a safe repeat.

  4. Record money in a balanced ledger

    Confirmation writes the order, marks the seat sold and appends ledger entries in one database transaction. Every movement is a set of entries that sum to zero. Mistakes are fixed by adding reversing entries, never by editing history.

5The hard part: two services, one outcomeThe hard part

Every interview has one part where the answer is not obvious. Spend your time here.

Seats live in your database; the card lives in the provider's system. You cannot wrap both in one transaction. Two phase commit would hold locks while waiting on an outside party and can block if the coordinator dies, so it is a poor fit. Use a saga: a chain of local steps (hold seat, charge card, confirm order), each with an undo (release seat, refund). If a step fails, run the undos for the steps already done.

The sharpest case is a payment timeout, because the outcome is unknown: the card may or may not have been charged. Do not release the seat or confirm yet. Record the payment as pending, then settle it from the provider's webhook or status check. A reconciliation job compares your records with the provider's, and alerts on any holds or pending payments that stay stuck.

1Overselling in a real database, then the conditional update

The race, then the fix, with real concurrent clients. In the naive version both buyers check that the seat is free, both see it free, and both update: both are told they succeeded and only the last write remains, an oversold seat. The conditional update puts the decision inside one atomic database statement: the first update changes one row, and the second finds sold_to already set and changes zero rows, so exactly one buyer is told sold out. Always make the *check* and the *change* a single operation.

2Retries replay one charge, and the ledger sums to zero

A payment as a ledger transaction, retried four times. The idempotency key order-81 is stored with the result, so attempts two to four return replayed txn-order-81 and charge nothing: Ana is debited once. Each payment writes balanced entries: Ana's debit equals the merchant's credit plus the fee, in one transaction, so the sum of every entry in the ledger is exactly zero. That invariant is what auditors and reconciliation jobs check: any non-zero total means money appeared or vanished. (A real system also passes the idempotency key to the payment provider and reconciles against their settlement files.)

create table accounts(id text primary key);
insert into accounts values ('customer:ana'), ('merchant:shop'), ('fees');
create table ledger(id bigserial primary key, txn text not null, account text not null references accounts, amount_cents bigint not null);
create table payments(idem_key text primary key, txn text not null, amount_cents bigint not null);
create function pay(k text, cust text, amt bigint) returns text language plpgsql as $$
declare t text;
begin
  select txn into t from payments where idem_key = k;
  if t is not null then return 'replayed ' || t; end if;                       -- a retry: return the stored result, charge nothing
  t := 'txn-' || k;
  insert into payments values (k, t, amt);
  insert into ledger(txn, account, amount_cents) values (t, cust, -amt), (t, 'merchant:shop', amt - amt / 50), (t, 'fees', amt / 50);   -- debit = credits
  return 'charged ' || t;
end $$;
EOF

3A saga that compensates when a step fails

A saga with no global lock. The happy path runs three local steps in order. In the failing run the card step fails, so the saga walks backwards through the completed steps and runs each compensation: the hotel room and then the seat are released. Each compensation is a new business action, not a database rollback, so the world passes through intermediate states (a seat was briefly held for someone who then did not pay), which is why sagas lack isolation and why real systems show "pending" and use reservations that expire.

python3 - <<'EOF'
# A saga: book flight, book hotel, charge card. Each step has a compensation; failures undo completed steps in reverse.
log = []
def step(name, action, undo, fail=False):
    return dict(name=name, action=action, undo=undo, fail=fail)
def run_saga(steps):
    done = []
    for s in steps:
        if s['fail']:
            log.append('%-14s FAILED' % s['name'])
            for d in reversed(done): log.append('%-14s compensate: %s' % (d['name'], d['undo']))
            return 'saga aborted: all earlier steps compensated'
        log.append('%-14s done:       %s' % (s['name'], s['action'])); done.append(s)
    return 'saga completed'
print(run_saga([step('book flight', 'seat 14C held', 'seat released'), step('book hotel', 'room 302 held', 'room released'), step('charge card', 'authorised 480', 'authorisation voided')]))
print('\n'.join('  ' + l for l in log)); log.clear()
print()
print(run_saga([step('book flight', 'seat 14C held', 'seat released'), step('book hotel', 'room 302 held', 'room released'), step('charge card', 'authorised 480', 'authorisation voided', fail=True)]))
print('\n'.join('  ' + l for l in log))
EOF

6Scale, failure and trade-offsDeep dive

A million people arrive at once

A waiting room admits users at a steady rate so the database sees a trickle, not a spike. Browsing is served from cache with slightly stale seat counts; the decision itself is never stale.

Hot event on one shard

Shard by event so every seat decision for one event lives in one place with strong consistency. Add event level rate limits and keep transactions short.

Provider times out

Mark the payment pending and settle it later by webhook or status call. Webhooks must be idempotent too, as providers send them more than once.

Stuck holds

A hold that never resolves blocks a seat. The sweeper frees expired holds and an alert fires if the count of old pending payments grows.

Bots and scalpers

Rate limit per account and per payment method, use challenges in the waiting room, and cap seats per order.

7Say it in two minutesThe answer

A commonly reported correctness question: reported by candidates, not official.

"Design a ticket booking system like Ticketmaster. How do you avoid double booking and handle payment failures?"

3. How to say it

A model answer, about two minutes aloud

"The browsing side is cacheable, but the booking side concentrates a million people on a few thousand rows, so I put a waiting room in front that admits users at a controlled rate. Seat allocation is decided by the database: a conditional update from available to held with an expiry, or a unique constraint, in one short transaction, never a check in the application followed by a separate update. A hold lasts ten minutes and expires automatically.

Payment uses an idempotency key per attempt. On success one transaction marks the seat sold and writes balanced ledger entries; on failure the hold expires or is released. A timeout from the provider is an unknown outcome, so I record the payment as pending and reconcile through the provider's status API or webhooks before confirming or releasing, with refunds as compensations in a saga. I shard by event so each hot event has one strongly consistent home, use caches and replicas for browsing, and monitor stuck holds, pending payments and the ledger invariant that all entries sum to zero."

4. Likely follow-ups

Pessimistic lock or conditional update?

A conditional update is simpler and holds no lock while the user decides; a row lock works for short critical sections but must never span the payment call.

What if the payment succeeds after the hold expired?

Check the seat inside the confirmation transaction: if it was sold to someone else, refund automatically (a compensation), and tell the buyer.

How do you stop bots?

Waiting room with proof of work or verified accounts, per-user and per-IP limits, purchase caps, and risk scoring.

Common slips

  • Checking availability in the application and updating separately.
  • Holding a database lock while waiting for payment.
  • Treating a payment timeout as a failure.

8Remember thisRecap

The page in 6 lines

  • Let the database decide: one conditional update from available to held, never check then write.
  • Holds carry an expiry, so abandoned seats free themselves.
  • A waiting room turns a million arrivals into a steady trickle.
  • An idempotency key makes payment retries safe: in the recorded run, 4 attempts and 1 charge.
  • A balanced ledger sums to zero; fix mistakes with reversing entries.
  • Seat plus payment is a saga with undo steps; a timeout means pending, not failed.

Check yourself

Why is check then update unsafe?

Two buyers can both see the seat free before either writes; the conditional update makes the check and the write one atomic step.

What does an idempotency key do?

It lets the server recognise a retry and replay the first result instead of charging again.

Why not two phase commit with the payment provider?

It holds locks across outside calls and can block on a coordinator failure; a saga with compensations avoids that.
