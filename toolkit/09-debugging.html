Chapter 9 of 14Night shift at ShopKart

Debug like a detective

Why guessing is the slowest way to fix a bug, and the simple loop that finds the cause of almost anything: observe, guess, predict, test.

about 20 minutes4 steps4 real experiments3 interview questions

109:07, the morning after launchThe story

The night before, ShopKart started shipping to New Zealand. At 09:07 you are about to hand over your first night shift when support pings: some customers cannot check out. The log shows three failed checkouts in three minutes, one of them slow (2,310 ms) just before it died.

Three people gather around one screen and five theories appear. Sam from support blames the payment provider. Maya, who wrote the release, says it cannot be her code. Someone says the database looks slow, someone else says the CDN, and a fifth person says it is the customers' browsers. Each theory is plausible. Nobody has checked any of them.

You ask for one thing: stop, and read the log around the first error. A few lines up from the failure is a traceback ending in KeyError: 'NZ'. The new pricing code has no entry for New Zealand. Four of the five theories were wrong, and it took forty minutes of arguing to find out what one search would have shown in forty seconds.

2The idea: a loop, not a hunchThe idea

Debugging is a loop of four moves: observe the evidence, form one hypothesis, predict what you would see if it is true, then test it. Each pass either finds the cause or removes a suspect.

It works because it replaces opinions with cheap experiments. Good debuggers are not cleverer; they change one thing at a time, write down what they tried, and trust evidence over memory. The Google SRE book describes troubleshooting in exactly these terms, as the hypothetico-deductive method: hypothesise a cause, test it, repeat.

3How it works, step by stepHow it works

  1. Observe before you guess

    Say exactly what happens, to whom, and since when. Read the error message in full and find the first error in the log, because the newest line is usually a side effect.

  2. Make a guess that can be proven wrong

    A hypothesis is one specific theory, such as "the price table has no entry for New Zealand". Add a prediction: if that is true, an Australian order should still work. A guess that nothing could disprove is not a test.

  3. Narrow it down by halving

    If you cannot see the cause, cut the problem in half and test one half. Good or bad tells you where the fault is not. Sixteen items take four tests, and a million take about twenty.

  4. Compare working with broken

    When something works in one place and not in another, stop reasoning and diff them: environment variables, versions, config files, the commit before and after. The difference is the shortlist of suspects.

5How it breaks in productionFailure modes

Changing five things at once

The problem goes away and you do not know which change fixed it, or whether you broke something else. Fix: change one thing, test, and undo it if it did nothing.

Fixing the symptom

You restart the server, the errors stop, and everyone goes home. The cause is still there and returns at the next peak. Fix: keep the evidence (logs, a trace) and find why before declaring it done.

Reading the last line, not the first

The newest error is often a knock-on effect of an earlier one. In the recorded log, three errors repeat the same failure, and the traceback sits after the first. Fix: find the first error and read around it.

Only testing your favourite theory

You look for evidence that supports your guess and ignore the rest. Fix: write the prediction first, and add what would prove you wrong.

Dismissing the flaky one

A job that fails 3 times in 12 runs is not bad luck, it is a bug with a rate. Fix: loop until it fails, keep that run's output, and study that exact run.

Waiting on a hang

A command that never returns gives no error to read. Fix: wrap it in timeout, which stops it and returns exit status 124, so a hang becomes a clear signal.

6How real companies do itReal companies

Google SRE

The chapter on effective troubleshooting describes troubleshooting as an application of the hypothetico-deductive method: form possible causes from what you observe, then test them by comparing the system with the hypothesis or by making a controlled change.

Google SRE book: Effective Troubleshooting
Git

The git bisect documentation describes using a binary search over history: start from a known good and a known bad commit, test the middle, and repeat until the first bad commit is found. It is the halving idea from this chapter, automated.

git documentation: git-bisect

7In the interviewTop-company questions

Say your answer aloud first, then compare with the model answer. These are the shapes of question candidates report, not official lists.

A very common troubleshooting prompt: reported by candidates at many companies, not official.

Walk me through how you debug a problem you have never seen before.

Reported by candidates at large tech companies as a common prompt (also in behavioural rounds); not official.

How to say it

A model answer, about two minutes aloud

Observe: exact symptom, when it started, who is affected, what changed.

Reproduce it with the smallest case; if I cannot, I add logging or metrics first.

Hypothesise, predict, test one change at a time, narrowing by halving; write notes.

Fix the cause, add a test or alert so it cannot come back silently, and write down what I learned.

Likely follow-ups

• What do you do when you cannot reproduce it?

• How do you decide between mitigating and finding the cause?

Common slips

  • Changing several things at once.
  • Guessing without a test.
A frequent on call question: reported by candidates, not official.

Production is down and you are on call. What is your first move?

Amazon-style: expect questions on customer impact first and root cause after; reported by candidates, not official.

How to say it

A model answer, about two minutes aloud

Check what changed recently and roll back if there is a clear suspect; stopping the harm comes first.

Communicate status early, assign roles for larger incidents, and keep a timeline.

Investigate the cause afterwards, calmly, and write a blameless postmortem.

Likely follow-ups

• How do you decide to roll back?

Common slips

  • Debugging live while users suffer when a rollback is available.
A popular follow-up about flaky tests: reported by candidates, not official.

A test fails one run in twenty. How do you handle it?

A common interview question on this topic.

How to say it

A model answer, about two minutes aloud

Measure the rate over many runs and capture a failing run with full logs.

Look for races, shared state, time, ordering, external dependencies and resource limits.

Make it fail more often (stress, reduced timeouts) to confirm the cause, then fix the cause rather than adding retries or sleeps.

Likely follow-ups

• When are retries acceptable?

Common slips

  • Re-running until green.

8Remember thisRecap

The page in 6 lines

  • Stop guessing: observe first, and say exactly what happens, to whom and since when.
  • Find the first error in the log and read the lines around it; read a stack trace from the bottom up.
  • A good hypothesis is specific and comes with a prediction that could prove it wrong.
  • Halve the problem: sixteen items take four tests, and the same trick finds bad commits and bad config.
  • When it works here and not there, diff the two environments instead of arguing about them.
  • Change one thing at a time, keep notes, and wrap anything that might hang in a timeout.

Check yourself

What are the four moves of the debugging loop?

Observe, hypothesise, predict, test. If the test disagrees with the prediction, you have new facts and loop again.

Why read a stack trace from the bottom?

The last line says what was raised, and the lines above it say which code raised it and how it got there.

How many tests does it take to find one bad line in 16?

Four, by testing half at a time: 16 to 8 to 4 to 2 to 1.
