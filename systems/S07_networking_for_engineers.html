Networking for engineers

Every outage story eventually says "it was the network" or "it was DNS". This module takes one request, from typing a URL to the page appearing, and slows it down until every step is visible: addressing, TCP, DNS, HTTP, TLS, real-time channels and proxies. Each part comes with a flow diagram, a real captured trace explained line by line, the ways it fails, and a worked interview question with the reasoning spelled out.

9 lessons4 labs4 animations8 interview questionsabout 8 to 10 hours

Why this matters

A real situation

Users report that the site is down. Is the name wrong, the port closed, the certificate expired, or the application returning errors? Each has a different fix and a one-line test. Engineers who know how a request really travels answer in two minutes; the rest guess for two hours.

Interviews ask "what happens when you type a URL into the browser?" because every answer reveals your depth: a junior lists four words, a senior walks the whole path, names where time is spent and says how each step fails.

The method used here is the one senior engineers use to learn anything unfamiliar: follow one concrete request from start to finish, look at the real bytes, and ask at each step "what could go wrong, and how would I see it?"

After this module you can
  • Explain, step by step, what happens from typing a URL to the page rendering, and where the time goes.
  • Read an IP address with a network mask, and explain ports, sockets and the layers a packet passes through.
  • Describe TCP (handshake, sequence numbers, retransmission, flow and congestion control, closing) and read a tcpdump capture.
  • Compare TCP and UDP and say when each is right.
  • Explain DNS resolution, record types, TTLs and caching, and read a dig trace.
  • Explain HTTP messages, status codes, keep-alive, caching headers and what HTTP/2 and HTTP/3 change.
  • Explain the TLS handshake, certificates and chains, and diagnose certificate errors.
  • Choose between polling, long polling, server-sent events and WebSockets, and explain reverse proxies.
  • Walk the troubleshooting ladder and answer network questions in interviews with structure and evidence.

The lessons

1

The whole journey: what happens when you open a URL

Must know
Situation

You type https://www.example.com/ and press Enter. Half a second later a page appears. Between those two moments at least five different systems did work, and any of them can be the reason it is slow or broken.

You will learn
  • The five stages and what each one does
  • Where the time goes (and how to measure it)
  • Why this is the backbone of every networking question
Mental model

Think of one request as a journey with five stages: find the address (DNS), open a connection (TCP), make it private and verified (TLS), ask and answer (HTTP), use the answer (render, then repeat for every image and script). The rest of this module opens each stage in turn. The most useful habit is to keep this map in your head: when something is slow or broken, the question is always "which stage?"

Everyday analogy: Sending a parcel: look up the address (DNS), get the door opened (TCP), check the recipient's ID (TLS), hand over the parcel and get a receipt (HTTP), then unpack it (render).

The picture
How it works, step by step
  1. The browser checks its own memory firstIf it visited this site recently it may already know the IP address, even have the page cached. Nothing leaves your machine. This is why second visits feel faster.
  2. DNS: name to addressComputers connect to numbers (IP addresses), people type names. The browser asks a DNS resolver (usually your router or your provider) for the address of www.example.com. If the resolver does not know, it walks down the DNS hierarchy and remembers the answer for a limited time.
  3. TCP: open a connectionTo the address it just learned, on port 443, the browser opens a reliable channel: SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK. This costs one full round trip, so the distance to the server matters before a single byte of your page moves.
  4. TLS: privacy and identityBoth sides agree on secret keys and the server proves who it is with a certificate signed by an authority the browser trusts. In TLS 1.3 this adds one more round trip.
  5. HTTP: ask and answerNow the browser sends GET / HTTP/1.1 with a Host header and receives a status line, headers and the HTML. The status code is the first clue: 2xx fine, 3xx redirect, 4xx you asked wrongly, 5xx the server failed.
  6. Render, and repeatThe browser reads the HTML, finds more files (stylesheets, scripts, images), and repeats stages 4 (and sometimes 2 to 4) for each, often over the same open connection. A page is dozens of requests, not one.

Interview in practice

"What happens when you type a URL into the browser and press Enter?"

The question: You are asked this in the first ten minutes of an interview. How do you answer so the interviewer sees depth, not a list of words?

Pause for a minute and say how you would start, then compare with the walkthrough below.

1. Clarify first (what to ask, and why it matters)
  • Do you want the network path or the whole page load? Decide the scope out loud. Say you will cover the network path in detail and mention rendering briefly.
  • HTTP or HTTPS, and a first visit or a repeat visit? Assume HTTPS and a first visit: that shows every stage. Mention what caches change afterwards.
2. Think it through (the reasoning, step by step)
  1. Draw the map first. Say "five stages: DNS, TCP, TLS, HTTP, render" and draw them as boxes. The interviewer now knows where you are heading and can steer you.
  2. Go stage by stage, always answering three things: what it does, what it costs, how it fails. DNS turns a name into an address (cost: 0 to a few round trips, cached by TTL; failure: NXDOMAIN or timeout). TCP opens a reliable connection (cost: one round trip; failure: refused or timed out). TLS agrees keys and verifies identity (cost: one more round trip; failure: certificate errors). HTTP is request and response (cost: server time; failure: 4xx and 5xx). Render fetches more resources and repeats.
  3. Name where the time goes. On a cold request the round trips add up (DNS, TCP, TLS, request); that is why connection reuse, CDNs near users and HTTP/2 and HTTP/3 exist.
  4. Mention the caches at every level. Browser cache, operating-system DNS cache, resolver cache, CDN edge cache. This is the point where most answers are missing something.
  5. Finish with how you would debug it. "If the page does not load I walk the same path with commands: getent hosts, nc -zv, openssl s_client, curl -v." That shows you use the knowledge.
3. A strong answer, said out loud

"Typing the URL starts five stages. First the browser checks its caches; if it does not know the address it asks a DNS resolver, which walks the DNS hierarchy and caches the answer for its TTL. Then it opens a TCP connection to that address on port 443 with the three-way handshake, which costs one round trip. For HTTPS a TLS handshake follows: the server presents a certificate signed by a trusted authority, both sides derive session keys, and from then on everything is encrypted; with TLS 1.3 that is one extra round trip.

Then the browser sends an HTTP request with the method, path and Host header and gets back a status code, headers and the body. The browser parses the HTML, discovers more resources and fetches them, usually reusing the connection and, with HTTP/2 or 3, multiplexing many requests on it.

On a cold start the cost is roughly three round trips before the request even goes out, which is why latency to the server, CDNs, keep-alive and TLS session resumption matter. And when something is slow I measure each stage with curl -w, and when it is broken I test DNS, TCP, TLS and HTTP in that order and stop at the first failure."

4. Follow-ups you should expect
Where does a CDN fit in?

At the DNS step: the name resolves to an address of a server near you (an edge). That edge answers from its cache or fetches from the origin, so the TCP, TLS and HTTP round trips are short because the server is close.

What changes on the second visit?

DNS is usually cached, the connection may be reused or resumed, and the browser may serve files from its cache or revalidate them with If-None-Match and get a tiny 304.

Which stage is the slowest on average?

It depends, which is the honest answer: on a good connection DNS and TCP are tens of milliseconds, TLS adds a similar amount, and the server's own processing (time to first byte) is often the largest part. Measure instead of guessing.

Common slips
  • Listing "DNS, TCP, HTTP" and stopping: no TLS, no caches, no costs.
  • Saying DNS "takes you to the server": DNS only returns an address; the connection is a separate step.
  • Not saying how you would find which stage is failing.
Quick check

Answer in your head first, then open it.

Name the five stages of loading a page.

DNS lookup, TCP connect, TLS handshake, HTTP request and response, then render and repeat for more resources.

In curl's timing output, how do you find the time spent in the TLS handshake?

Subtract the TCP-connected time from the TLS-finished time; all curl timings are measured from the start.

Traps to avoid (2)
  • Treating the network as instantaneous: a cold HTTPS request needs about three round trips before the request is sent.
  • Blaming "the network" when the time is actually in the server (a large gap before the first byte).

Addresses, subnets, ports and layers

Must know
Situation

Two programs on different machines must exchange bytes. Something has to say which machine, which program on it, and how to package the data for the trip across many networks.

You will learn
  • IP addresses and the network mask (CIDR)
  • Ports and sockets
  • Layers and encapsulation: why every device only reads its own envelope
Mental model

An IP address (like 192.168.10.77) names a network interface. A network mask splits it into a *network part* and a *host part*: 192.168.10.0/24 means the first 24 bits are the network, so the 8 remaining bits give 256 addresses in that network. Machines in the same network talk directly; for anything else they send packets to a gateway (router), which forwards them towards the destination. A port (0 to 65535) names a program on the machine; the pair address:port is a socket address, and a connection is identified by four numbers: source IP and port, destination IP and port.

Data is wrapped in layers like envelopes inside envelopes. The application produces bytes (an HTTP request). TCP adds ports and sequence numbers. IP adds the source and destination addresses; routers read only this envelope. The link layer (Ethernet or Wi-Fi) adds the hardware address of the next device on this one network and is thrown away at each hop. Each layer only trusts and reads its own envelope, which is why you can swap Wi-Fi for fibre without touching HTTP.

Everyday analogy: A letter: the street address is the IP, the apartment number is the port, the postcode helps the sorting office decide the next town (the mask and routing), and each van uses only the label for its leg of the journey (the link layer).

How it works, step by step
  1. Is the destination on my own network?Your machine applies your network mask to the destination address. If the network parts match, it talks to that machine directly. If not, it sends the packet to the default gateway.
  2. The gateway forwards it, hop by hopEach router looks only at the destination IP, picks the next hop from its routing table and passes the packet on. No router knows the whole route, only the next step.
  3. NAT hides private addressesHome and office networks use private ranges (10.x.x.x, 172.16-31.x.x, 192.168.x.x). The router rewrites the source address to its one public address and remembers the mapping so replies find their way back.
  4. The destination host picks the programThe packet arrives at the right machine; the destination port tells the operating system which program (which socket) gets the data.

Interview in practice

"Explain the layers of the network stack and why they exist."

The question: An interviewer asks you to explain TCP/IP layers (or the OSI model) without reciting a table. How do you make it useful?

Pause for a minute and say how you would start, then compare with the walkthrough below.

1. Clarify first (what to ask, and why it matters)
  • Four layers (TCP/IP) or seven (OSI)? Say you will use the four-layer TCP/IP model because it matches how the internet is built, and mention OSI's seven as a finer teaching model.
2. Think it through (the reasoning, step by step)
  1. Start from the problem, not the names. Moving bytes between programs on different machines across many networks is too much for one piece of software, so it is split into layers that each solve one problem and hide it from the next.
  2. Give each layer one sentence and one example. Link: delivers a frame to the next device on this network (Ethernet, Wi-Fi, MAC addresses). Internet: gets packets across many networks using IP addresses and routers. Transport: gets data to the right program, optionally reliably (TCP) or not (UDP), using ports. Application: the meaning of the data (HTTP, DNS, TLS on top of TCP).
  3. Show encapsulation with a concrete request. An HTTP request is wrapped in a TCP segment, which is wrapped in an IP packet, which is wrapped in an Ethernet frame. At each hop the outer frame is replaced, while the IP packet and everything inside stay the same.
  4. Tie it to debugging. Layering tells you where to look: no link light is a layer 1/2 problem, no route is layer 3, a refused connection is layer 4, a 500 is layer 7.
  5. Mention the leaky cases. NAT rewrites addresses and ports (so routers do look above layer 3); load balancers can work at layer 4 or layer 7. Real networks are not perfectly layered.
3. A strong answer, said out loud

"Layering exists because moving data between programs across the internet is several different problems, and each layer solves one and hides it from the layer above. At the bottom the link layer gets a frame to the next device on the same network: Ethernet or Wi-Fi with hardware addresses. Above it the internet layer, IP, gets a packet across many networks using IP addresses; routers only read this envelope and forward by the next hop.

The transport layer, TCP or UDP, uses ports to deliver the data to the right program, and TCP also makes the delivery reliable and ordered. The application layer is the protocol the programs speak: HTTP, DNS, TLS and so on.

When I send an HTTP request, it is wrapped in a TCP segment, then an IP packet, then a link frame; each router strips and replaces the outer frame but leaves the IP packet intact. For debugging, the layers give me an order: is there a route, does the port accept connections, does the application answer correctly."

4. Follow-ups you should expect
Where do NAT and load balancers break the model?

NAT rewrites IP addresses and ports on the way, so it looks into layer 4. A layer 7 load balancer parses HTTP to route by path or header. They are practical shortcuts that ignore strict layering.

What is the difference between a switch, a router and a gateway?

A switch forwards frames inside one network using hardware addresses; a router forwards packets between networks using IP addresses; the default gateway is the router your machine sends non-local traffic to.

Common slips
  • Reciting seven layer names with no example of what lives at each.
  • Saying routers "read the whole packet": they read only the IP header to forward.
Quick check

Answer in your head first, then open it.

How many addresses does a /24 network contain, and how many are usable hosts?

256 addresses, 254 usable (network and broadcast addresses are reserved).

What four values identify a TCP connection?

Source IP, source port, destination IP, destination port.

Which layer's header do routers read to forward a packet?

The IP (internet layer) header.

Traps to avoid (2)
  • Confusing a service bound to 127.0.0.1 (this machine only) with one bound to 0.0.0.0 (every interface): the second may be reachable from outside.
  • Forgetting that private addresses are reused everywhere: two companies can both use 10.0.0.5, so they must never be exposed directly.

TCP and UDP: reliable streams and plain datagrams

Must know
Situation

The network between you and the server loses, duplicates and reorders packets. Yet a web page arrives intact. Something turned an unreliable network into a reliable pipe. That something is TCP.

You will learn
  • The three-way handshake and what the numbers do
  • Retransmission, flow control and congestion control
  • Closing a connection and TIME_WAIT
  • UDP and how to choose
Mental model

TCP gives a reliable, ordered stream of bytes. Every byte has a sequence number; the receiver sends acknowledgements saying how far it has got; if an acknowledgement does not arrive in time the sender retransmits. The handshake (SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK) agrees the starting numbers and costs one round trip. Flow control stops a fast sender drowning a slow receiver (the receiver advertises a window). Congestion control stops senders overwhelming the network: a new connection starts with a small window and doubles it every round trip (slow start) until loss tells it to back off. Closing is done with FIN in each direction, and the side that closes first waits in TIME_WAIT so stray packets cannot be mistaken for a new connection.

UDP is the opposite choice: independent datagrams with no connection, no ordering, no retransmission. It is lower in latency and overhead, and the application decides what to do about loss. DNS queries, video calls, games and QUIC (the base of HTTP/3) use it.

Everyday analogy: TCP is a phone call: you dial (handshake), confirm you are both there, each sentence is acknowledged ("yes, I got that"), and you say goodbye. UDP is shouting messages across a field: fast, no guarantees, and you decide what to do if one is missed.

The picture
How it works, step by step
  1. Handshake: agree where counting startsThe client sends SYN with a random starting sequence number x. The server replies SYN-ACK with its own starting number y and acknowledges x+1. The client acknowledges y+1. Both sides now know where the other one's numbering starts, and the connection is established.
  2. Data flows in numbered segmentsEach segment says "these are bytes N to M". The receiver acknowledges the next byte it expects. Acknowledgements are cumulative: "I have everything up to byte M".
  3. Loss is repaired by retransmissionIf the sender gets no acknowledgement within a timeout, or receives duplicate acknowledgements, it sends the missing segment again. The application never sees the loss, only some added delay.
  4. Flow control protects the receiverEvery acknowledgement carries a window: how many more bytes the receiver can buffer. A slow receiver shrinks the window; the sender must slow down.
  5. Congestion control protects the networkThe sender keeps its own limit (the congestion window). It starts small, doubles each round trip in slow start, then grows gently, and halves (or more) when it detects loss. This is why a new connection is slow at first and why long-lived connections are faster.
  6. Closing: FIN, ACK, TIME_WAITEach side sends FIN when it has no more data. The side that closed first stays in TIME_WAIT for a short time (about a minute on many systems) to absorb late packets. Thousands of TIME_WAIT sockets on a busy server are normal.

Interview in practice

"TCP versus UDP: when would you use each, and what does TCP actually guarantee?"

The question: Explain the difference, give real examples, and say what TCP's guarantees cost.

Pause for a minute and say how you would start, then compare with the walkthrough below.

1. Clarify first (what to ask, and why it matters)
  • Do you want protocol details or design guidance? Cover both briefly: what each does, then how you choose.
2. Think it through (the reasoning, step by step)
  1. State the guarantee precisely. TCP delivers every byte, in order, exactly once to the application, or reports an error. It does this with sequence numbers, acknowledgements, retransmission, and checksums.
  2. State the cost. The handshake (one round trip), head-of-line blocking (one lost segment delays everything behind it until it is retransmitted), connection state on both ends, and congestion control that starts slow.
  3. Describe UDP as the absence of those features, not as a worse TCP. It adds only ports and a checksum. The application chooses: drop late data, resend selectively, or accept loss.
  4. Give examples of each with the reason. TCP: web, SSH, email, databases (correctness over latency). UDP: DNS queries (one small question, one small answer, retry is cheap), live video and voice (a late packet is useless, so retransmitting is pointless), games, and QUIC, which builds reliability and streams on top of UDP so it can avoid TCP's head-of-line blocking.
  5. Show you know the nuance. Most traffic you meet is TCP. Choosing UDP means you must handle loss, ordering, congestion, and abuse (UDP is easy to spoof for amplification attacks) yourself.
3. A strong answer, said out loud

"TCP gives the application a reliable, ordered byte stream: it numbers every byte, the receiver acknowledges, and the sender retransmits what is not acknowledged, so the application sees every byte once and in order. It also has flow control so a fast sender does not overwhelm a slow receiver, and congestion control so it does not flood the network. The costs are a round trip for the handshake, connection state, a slow start on new connections and head-of-line blocking when a segment is lost.

UDP just sends independent datagrams with ports and a checksum: no connection, no retransmission, no ordering. It is the right base when low latency matters more than every packet, or when a retry is trivial: DNS queries, voice and video calls, online games. QUIC, which carries HTTP/3, is an example of building reliability on top of UDP to get faster connection setup and avoid head-of-line blocking across streams.

My default is TCP; I would choose UDP only if I can explain how my application deals with loss and ordering."

4. Follow-ups you should expect
What is head-of-line blocking?

In TCP, if segment 5 is lost, segments 6 to 10 that already arrived cannot be handed to the application until 5 is retransmitted, because the stream must stay in order. With HTTP/2 over TCP this stalls every multiplexed request; HTTP/3 over QUIC avoids it by making streams independent.

Why does DNS use UDP?

A query and answer each fit in one small packet, so there is no benefit from a connection; if a packet is lost the client just asks again. DNS switches to TCP for large answers.

What is a SYN flood?

An attack that sends many SYNs without completing the handshake, filling the server's table of half-open connections. Defences include SYN cookies and rate limiting.

Common slips
  • "UDP is unreliable so it is bad": it is a different trade-off, chosen for latency.
  • Saying TCP guarantees speed or delivery time: it guarantees order and correctness, not latency.
Quick check

Answer in your head first, then open it.

What do the three packets of the TCP handshake accomplish?

They agree starting sequence numbers on both sides and confirm both can send and receive; it costs one round trip.

Why is a new TCP connection slow at first?

Congestion control starts with a small window and doubles it each round trip (slow start) until it finds the network's capacity.

What does TIME_WAIT protect against?

Late packets from an old connection being mistaken for part of a new one on the same address and port pair.

Traps to avoid (3)
  • Believing more bandwidth fixes slow small transfers: round trips and slow start dominate them.
  • Thousands of TIME_WAIT sockets are normal on a busy client or proxy; thousands of CLOSE_WAIT sockets mean the application forgot to close.
  • Treating UDP "success" as delivery: it only means the packet was handed to the network.

DNS: turning names into addresses

Must know
Situation

The site works by IP address but not by name, works for you but not for a colleague, or a change you made yesterday is still not visible. All of these are DNS stories.

You will learn
  • The resolution chain, step by step
  • Record types and what each is for
  • TTLs, caching and why changes "propagate"
  • Reading a real dig +trace
Mental model

DNS is a distributed, hierarchical database that maps names to data. Nobody holds the whole thing: the root servers know who runs each top-level domain (.com), the TLD servers know who runs each domain (example.com), and the domain's authoritative servers hold the actual records. Your machine rarely does this walk itself: it asks a recursive resolver (your router, ISP or a public one), which does the walk and caches every answer for its TTL (time to live, in seconds). Common records: A (name to IPv4), AAAA (IPv6), CNAME (alias to another name), MX (mail), NS (which servers are authoritative), TXT (arbitrary text, used for verification and email security).

Everyday analogy: Asking directory enquiries: you ask your local operator (the resolver). If she does not know, she asks the national directory (root), who points her to the regional one (TLD), who points her to the office that keeps the number (authoritative). She then writes the number on a sticky note for the next hour (the TTL).

The picture
How it works, step by step
  1. The application asks the system resolverBefore any network traffic, the operating system checks /etc/hosts and its own cache. /etc/nsswitch.conf says in which order sources are tried (usually files dns).
  2. The recursive resolver checks its cacheIf any resolver on the path has the answer and the TTL has not expired, it answers immediately. Most lookups end here, in a millisecond or two.
  3. Otherwise it asks a root serverThe root does not know www.example.com, but it knows who runs .com and replies with a referral: the TLD name servers.
  4. Then the TLD serverIt does not know the host either, but it knows which name servers are authoritative for example.com and refers the resolver to them.
  5. Finally the authoritative server answersIt holds the real records and returns, for example, an A record with a TTL of 300.
  6. The answer is cached and returnedEvery resolver along the way stores it for the TTL. This is the reason DNS changes take time to be seen everywhere: caches keep the old answer until their copy expires.

Interview in practice

"I changed a DNS record an hour ago and some users still see the old site. Why, and what do you do?"

The question: A classic operational interview question that tests whether you understand caching, TTLs and the resolution chain.

Pause for a minute and say how you would start, then compare with the walkthrough below.

1. Clarify first (what to ask, and why it matters)
  • What exactly did you change, and what was the TTL on the old record? The old TTL decides the worst case: caches may serve the old answer for that long after your change.
  • Is it all users or some, and are they on different networks? Some users means different resolvers with different cache states, which points at caching rather than a broken change.
2. Think it through (the reasoning, step by step)
  1. Confirm the change is correct at the source. Ask the authoritative servers directly: dig @ns1.example-dns.com www.example.com. If they return the new value, your change is live and the problem is only caches.
  2. Explain why some users are fine and some are not. Every resolver cached the old record when it last asked. Those whose copy has expired have fetched the new value; the others will until their TTL runs out. There is no push or "propagation": the new value is available immediately, copies simply expire on their own clocks.
  3. Bound the time. Worst case is the old TTL (say 24 hours, 86,400 s) from the moment each resolver last fetched. Check with dig against a few public resolvers to see remaining TTLs.
  4. Look for other caches. Browser, operating system, application runtime (some caches DNS forever, like a long-lived JVM), a corporate proxy, and negative caching (a recent NXDOMAIN may be cached too).
  5. Prevent it next time. Lower the TTL (for example to 60 s) a day or two before a planned change, make the change, then raise the TTL again. For fast failover use health-checked DNS or a load balancer instead of waiting on TTLs.
3. A strong answer, said out loud

"The change is probably already correct at the authoritative servers, and I would verify that first by querying them directly with dig. What users see depends on resolver caches: each resolver keeps the old record until its TTL expires, so different users see the change at different times. It is not propagation in the sense of a push; every copy simply expires on its own schedule, at most one old TTL after it last fetched the record.

So I would check the old TTL to know the worst case, query a couple of public resolvers to see what they still hold, and consider other caches such as browsers, the operating system and long-running applications that cache DNS themselves. To speed it up there is not much to do except wait or flush resolvers I control.

To avoid it next time I lower the TTL a day before a planned change, make the switch, and raise it again afterwards. For failover that must be quick I would not rely on DNS TTLs at all."

4. Follow-ups you should expect
What is negative caching?

Resolvers also cache "this name does not exist" (NXDOMAIN) for a time set in the zone's SOA record, so a record you just created may stay invisible for a while to those who asked just before.

Why can't you put a CNAME at the root of a domain?

The root of a zone must hold SOA and NS records, and a CNAME cannot coexist with other records at the same name. Providers offer ALIAS or ANAME records that behave like a CNAME but are resolved by the DNS provider.

How does DNS-based load balancing work and what are its limits?

The name returns several addresses or different ones by location or health. Limits: clients and resolvers cache answers, so failover is bounded by TTL and some clients ignore it.

Common slips
  • Saying "DNS takes up to 48 hours to propagate" without knowing it depends on TTLs.
  • Not checking the authoritative server before blaming caches (or the other way round).
Quick check

Answer in your head first, then open it.

Who answers the question "which servers are authoritative for example.com?"

The .com TLD servers, by referral; the authoritative servers themselves hold the actual records.

HTTP: the conversation your application has

Must know
Situation

An API call fails and the log says only "error". With curl and a clear picture of what HTTP messages look like, you can see exactly what was sent and what came back.

You will learn
  • The anatomy of a request and a response
  • Methods, status codes and idempotency
  • Keep-alive, caching headers, cookies
  • HTTP/1.1 versus HTTP/2 versus HTTP/3
Mental model

HTTP is a text protocol over a connection. A request has a request line (GET /v1/items HTTP/1.1), headers (Host, Accept, Authorization, Cookie...), a blank line and an optional body. A response has a status line (HTTP/1.1 200 OK), headers (Content-Type, Content-Length, Cache-Control, Set-Cookie...), a blank line and the body. Methods: GET reads, POST creates or triggers, PUT replaces, PATCH updates part, DELETE removes. GET, PUT and DELETE are idempotent (repeating them gives the same result), POST is not, which matters for retries. Status codes group by first digit: 2xx success, 3xx redirect (look at Location), 4xx the request was wrong (401 not authenticated, 403 forbidden, 404 not found, 429 too many requests), 5xx the server failed (500 bug, 502 bad gateway, 503 unavailable, 504 gateway timeout).

HTTP is stateless: every request stands alone, so state (who you are) is carried in cookies or tokens. Keep-alive reuses one connection for many requests, avoiding the handshake cost each time. HTTP/2 multiplexes many requests over one connection and compresses headers, but still sits on TCP so one lost packet stalls all streams. HTTP/3 runs over QUIC (on UDP) with independent streams and faster setup.

Everyday analogy: Filling in forms at a counter: the request is a form with a clear request line and attached notes (headers), the reply is a slip with a coded verdict (status) and the contents (body). Keep-alive is staying at the counter instead of queuing again for each form.

How it works, step by step
  1. The client opens (or reuses) a connectionWith keep-alive an existing connection is reused, so the DNS, TCP and TLS stages cost nothing for the second and later requests.
  2. It sends the request line and headersThe Host header is mandatory in HTTP/1.1: one IP can serve many sites, and Host says which one is wanted.
  3. The server routes and handles itA web server, framework or proxy maps the method and path to code, which may call databases and other services.
  4. The server answers with status, headers, bodyContent-Length or chunked encoding says where the body ends; Content-Type says how to read it; caching headers say how long it may be reused.
  5. The connection stays open (or closes)Connection: close or an idle timeout ends it; otherwise the next request reuses it.

Interview in practice

"What is the difference between HTTP/1.1, HTTP/2 and HTTP/3, and why did they change?"

The question: Explain what problem each version solved, as a story of one problem leading to the next fix.

Pause for a minute and say how you would start, then compare with the walkthrough below.

1. Clarify first (what to ask, and why it matters)
  • Are you looking for protocol details or the practical effect? Say you will explain the problem each version addressed, then the practical effect for latency and servers.
2. Think it through (the reasoning, step by step)
  1. HTTP/1.1: text, one request at a time per connection. Keep-alive fixed the cost of a new connection per request, but on one connection requests are answered in order, so a slow response blocks those behind it (head-of-line blocking at the HTTP level). Browsers worked around it by opening about six connections per host, and developers by bundling files and sharding domains.
  2. HTTP/2: multiplexing over one connection. Binary framing lets many requests and responses interleave as streams on one connection, with header compression (HPACK) and server push. One connection, no workarounds needed.
  3. But HTTP/2 still sits on TCP. TCP delivers one ordered byte stream, so a single lost packet stalls every stream until it is retransmitted: head-of-line blocking at the TCP level. On lossy networks (mobile) HTTP/2 can be worse than expected.
  4. HTTP/3: QUIC over UDP. QUIC implements reliability per stream on top of UDP, so a lost packet only stalls its own stream. It also merges the transport and TLS 1.3 handshakes, so a new connection needs one round trip (or zero when resuming), and connections survive a change of network (Wi-Fi to mobile) via connection IDs.
  5. Practical effect. Fewer round trips, better behaviour on lossy links, and simpler front-end code; the cost is more complex servers and middleboxes that must understand UDP-based QUIC.
3. A strong answer, said out loud

"Each version fixed the main bottleneck of the previous one. HTTP/1.1 sends one request at a time per connection, so a slow response blocks the ones behind it; keep-alive avoided reconnecting, and browsers opened several connections in parallel as a workaround. HTTP/2 multiplexes many requests as independent streams over a single connection and compresses headers, so those workarounds are no longer needed.

But HTTP/2 still runs on TCP, which delivers one ordered stream: one lost packet delays all streams until it is retransmitted. HTTP/3 therefore runs on QUIC over UDP, where reliability is per stream, so loss only affects the stream it hit. QUIC also builds TLS 1.3 into the handshake, so a new connection costs one round trip, and connections survive IP changes.

In practice I enable HTTP/2 or 3 at the CDN or load balancer and still design the application as if each request might be slow or fail."

4. Follow-ups you should expect
What is ALPN?

Application-Layer Protocol Negotiation: during the TLS handshake the client lists the protocols it supports (h2, http/1.1) and the server picks one, so the version is agreed without an extra round trip.

What is idempotency and why does it matter for HTTP?

An operation is idempotent if repeating it has the same effect as doing it once. GET, PUT and DELETE are; POST is not. Clients and proxies may safely retry idempotent requests after a timeout, but not POST unless you add an idempotency key.

How do cookies and sessions work over a stateless protocol?

The server sends Set-Cookie with a session ID or token; the browser sends it back in a Cookie header on every later request, so the server can recognise the user. Attributes like HttpOnly, Secure and SameSite limit how it can be stolen or misused.

Common slips
  • Saying HTTP/2 "is faster" with no mechanism.
  • Forgetting that HTTP/2 over TCP still has head-of-line blocking.

TLS and certificates: privacy and identity

Must know
Situation

curl says "SSL certificate problem". Someone suggests -k to make it go away. Why is that dangerous, and what is actually wrong?

You will learn
  • What TLS guarantees
  • The handshake, step by step
  • Certificates, authorities and the chain of trust
  • Reading a real server's certificate
Mental model

TLS gives a connection three things: confidentiality (nobody on the path can read it), integrity (nobody can change it unnoticed) and authentication (you are really talking to the named server). The first two come from session keys that both sides derive during the handshake without ever sending them. The third comes from the certificate: a signed statement that "this public key belongs to example.com". It is signed by a certificate authority (CA); your system ships a list of CAs it trusts (the trust store). Servers send a chain: the site's certificate, signed by an intermediate, signed by a root already in your trust store. The client checks that the chain leads to a trusted root, that the name matches, that the dates are valid, and that the server can prove it holds the private key.

Everyday analogy: A passport check: the passport (certificate) was issued by an authority you trust (CA), the border officer checks the photo (the server proves it holds the private key), the name and the expiry date. Encryption is then the sealed envelope you use once identity is settled.

The picture
How it works, step by step
  1. ClientHelloThe client lists the TLS versions and ciphers it supports, sends its half of a key exchange (a key share) and the server name it wants (SNI), so one IP can serve many certificates.
  2. ServerHelloThe server picks the version and cipher and sends its key share. At this point both sides can compute the same shared secret, using a Diffie-Hellman exchange, without it ever crossing the wire.
  3. Everything after this is encryptedIn TLS 1.3 even the certificate is sent encrypted. The server sends its certificate chain and a signature over the handshake made with its private key.
  4. The client verifiesIs the chain signed up to a trusted root? Does the certificate cover this name (the Subject Alternative Names)? Is it within its validity dates? Does the signature prove possession of the private key?
  5. Finished messagesEach side sends a keyed hash of the whole handshake to prove no one tampered with it. Then encrypted application data (the HTTP request) flows.

Real-time channels: polling, SSE and WebSockets

Must know
Situation

A chat message, a stock price or a delivery location must reach the screen the moment it changes. Plain HTTP only answers when asked, so how does a server tell a client something?

You will learn
  • Polling and long polling
  • Server-sent events (SSE)
  • WebSockets and the upgrade handshake
  • How to choose, and what it costs at scale
Mental model

HTTP is request-driven: the server cannot speak first. Four ways to get "live" data, in rising order of capability: Polling: ask every few seconds (simple, wasteful: most answers are "nothing new"). Long polling: ask, and the server holds the request open until it has something, then the client asks again (near real time, still one request per message). Server-sent events (SSE): one long-lived HTTP response that the server keeps writing events into (one direction, server to client; reconnects automatically; works through ordinary HTTP infrastructure). WebSockets: an HTTP request that upgrades the connection into a two-way channel carrying small frames in both directions (best for chat and games). The price of the last three is many long-lived connections: a server must hold hundreds of thousands of them, and load balancers, timeouts and reconnect storms need design.

Everyday analogy: Polling is checking your letterbox every five minutes. Long polling is asking the postman to wait at your door until a letter arrives. SSE is a radio broadcast you listen to. A WebSocket is a phone call where either side can speak at any time.

The picture
How it works, step by step
  1. PollingThe client repeats GET /updates every N seconds. Latency is up to N seconds and most responses are empty, but it works everywhere and is trivial to scale and cache.
  2. Long pollingThe server does not answer until an event exists (or a timeout of about 30 seconds). The client immediately re-asks. Latency is low, and each message still costs a full request and response.
  3. Server-sent eventsThe client opens GET /events with Accept: text/event-stream; the server keeps the response open and writes data: ... lines as events occur. Only server to client; the browser reconnects and resumes from the last event ID.
  4. WebSocket handshakeThe client sends an HTTP request with Upgrade: websocket and a random Sec-WebSocket-Key. The server replies 101 Switching Protocols with Sec-WebSocket-Accept computed from the key. From then on the same TCP connection carries framed messages both ways.
  5. Keep it alive and handle dropsBoth sides send periodic ping frames; clients reconnect with backoff and jitter and resync missed messages, because connections will drop.

Proxies and gateways: who is really talking to whom

Must know
Situation

Your service logs show every request coming from one IP, and the TLS certificate is not on your application at all. Between the client and your code sits a proxy, doing real work.

You will learn
  • Forward proxy versus reverse proxy
  • What a reverse proxy does for you
  • The headers that carry the real client identity
Mental model

A forward proxy sits in front of clients and makes requests on their behalf (a company filtering or caching web access, or a VPN-like tool). A reverse proxy sits in front of servers: clients think they are talking to your site, and the proxy picks a backend, forwards the request and returns the answer. Reverse proxies (nginx, HAProxy, Envoy, cloud load balancers, CDNs) do TLS termination (decrypting at the edge so backends speak plain HTTP), load balancing, caching, compression, rate limiting, authentication and routing by path or host. Because the backend now sees the proxy's address as the client, the proxy adds headers such as X-Forwarded-For (the real client IP, and each proxy appends the previous hop), X-Forwarded-Proto and Host. Applications must read those (only from trusted proxies) to know who really called.

Everyday analogy: A hotel receptionist (reverse proxy): guests do not walk into the kitchen; the receptionist takes the request, passes it to the right department, and returns the answer. A forward proxy is a personal assistant who makes calls for you so nobody sees your number.

The picture
How it works, step by step
  1. The client connects to the proxyDNS for your site points at the proxy (or a CDN or load balancer), not at your servers. TLS usually ends here, so the certificate lives on the proxy.
  2. The proxy chooses a backendBy health, load or request details (path, host, headers). A dead backend is skipped using health checks.
  3. It forwards the request, adding identity headersThe backend sees the proxy's IP as the peer, so the proxy adds X-Forwarded-For: client IP and X-Forwarded-Proto: https.
  4. The response comes back through the proxyThe proxy may cache it, compress it or rewrite headers before returning it to the client.

2. Think it through (the reasoning, step by step)
  1. Define by who each one hides. A forward proxy represents clients to the internet; a reverse proxy represents servers to clients. Same mechanism, opposite side.
  2. List what a reverse proxy gives. One public address and certificate for many backends; load balancing and health checks; caching and compression; rate limiting and WAF rules; routing by host and path; zero-downtime deploys by draining and swapping backends.
  3. Decide where TLS terminates. At the edge (proxy or CDN) is the common choice: certificates managed in one place, backends simpler and faster, and the proxy can inspect traffic for routing and security. Re-encrypt to the backend (or use mutual TLS) when the internal network is not fully trusted or compliance demands encryption in transit everywhere.
  4. Raise the caveats. The proxy is now a critical dependency and single point of failure (run several), it sees plaintext, and the application must trust forwarded headers only from known proxies, or clients can fake their IP.
3. A strong answer, said out loud

"A forward proxy sits in front of clients and makes requests for them, for example a company proxy that filters or caches outbound web traffic. A reverse proxy sits in front of servers: clients talk to the proxy, which chooses a healthy backend, forwards the request and returns the response. That gives me one address and certificate for many servers, load balancing with health checks, caching, compression, rate limiting and routing by path or host, and lets me deploy by swapping backends without clients noticing.

I would normally terminate TLS at the reverse proxy or CDN so certificates are managed in one place and backends stay simple, and re-encrypt to the backend if the internal network is not trusted or we have a compliance requirement. The caveats are that the proxy is a critical component, so I run it redundantly, and that the application must take the client IP from X-Forwarded-For only when it was set by our own proxies."

4. Follow-ups you should expect
What is the difference between L4 and L7 load balancing?

Layer 4 balances TCP connections by IP and port without reading the content: fast and simple. Layer 7 understands HTTP, so it can route by path or header, terminate TLS, and make smarter decisions at higher cost.

How can a client spoof its IP and how do you defend?

A client can send its own X-Forwarded-For. Trust only the entries appended by proxies you control (count hops from the right), or have the edge overwrite the header.

What is a CDN in these terms?

A globally distributed set of reverse proxies with caches, placed near users, in front of your origin.

Common slips
  • Mixing up forward and reverse proxies.
  • Trusting X-Forwarded-For from anyone without checking it came from a known proxy.
Quick check

Answer in your head first, then open it.

Who does a reverse proxy hide: clients or servers?

Servers: clients talk to the proxy, which forwards requests to the backends.

Why does the backend see the proxy's address as the client, and how is the real client identified?

The TCP connection comes from the proxy; the proxy adds X-Forwarded-For with the original client address.

What does TLS termination mean?

The proxy decrypts HTTPS at the edge and talks plain HTTP (or re-encrypted HTTP) to the backends.

Traps to avoid (3)
  • Trusting X-Forwarded-For from the open internet: it can be forged.
  • Health checks that only test the port, not the application, keep sending traffic to a broken backend.
  • One proxy instance in front of everything is a single point of failure.

Debugging the network: the ladder and what each failure looks like

Must know
Situation

"The site is down." You now know the whole path. The final skill is walking it in order with one command per stage, and recognising each failure by its message.

You will learn
  • The ladder: DNS, TCP, TLS, HTTP
  • What each failure message means
  • Comparing a working path with a broken one
Mental model

Because a request passes through stages in order, a failure message tells you the stage. Cannot resolve host is DNS. Connection refused means the machine answered but nothing listens (TCP). Connection timed out means packets vanish: firewall, wrong route or dead host (TCP). Certificate errors are TLS. 4xx and 5xx are HTTP and the application. Always test from the first stage downwards and stop at the first failure, then compare with a working client to decide whether the problem is the client, the path or the server.

Everyday analogy: A doctor works from the most basic checks (pulse, breathing) to the specific ones, in a fixed order, so nothing is missed and nothing is guessed.

The picture
How it works, step by step
  1. DNS: getent hosts nameNo answer means a resolver, record or typo problem. An answer that differs from what you expect means stale caches or a wrong record.
  2. TCP: nc -zv host portRefused: nothing is listening on that port (service down, wrong port). Timed out: packets are dropped (firewall, security group, routing). Success: the network path is fine.
  3. TLS: openssl s_client -connect host:443 -servername hostCheck the chain, the dates and the verification result. This isolates certificate problems from application problems.
  4. HTTP: curl -sv https://host/pathThe status code and headers show whether the application, a proxy or a gateway answered, and the timing shows where the time goes.
  5. Compare and narrow downRun the same four steps from a working machine. If it works there, the problem is the client or its network; if it fails everywhere, it is the server or a shared dependency.
Four rungs, one local service 
cd ~/net && (python3 -m http.server 8097 --bind 127.0.0.1 >/dev/null 2>&1 &); sleep 1; echo '--- DNS'; getent hosts localhost | head -1; echo '--- TCP'; nc -zv 127.0.0.1 8097 2>&1; echo '--- HTTP'; curl -s -o /dev/null -w 'status %{http_code}\n' http://127.0.0.1:8097/; echo '--- a closed port'; nc -zv 127.0.0.1 8998 2>&1; echo "exit status: $?"; pkill -f 'http.server 8097'

All the rungs for one local URL. The name resolves, the port accepts connections and HTTP returns 200 (there is no TLS rung because this server speaks plain HTTP). The last part shows how a failure reads: connecting to a port nobody listens on is refused immediately.

Interview in practice

"Users say the website is down. Walk me through how you troubleshoot it from the command line."

The question: The most common operational networking question. They want order, evidence and calm.

Pause for a minute and say how you would start, then compare with the walkthrough below.

1. Clarify first (what to ask, and why it matters)
  • Is it down for everyone or for some users? Since when? What changed? Scope and timing steer everything: all users and a recent deploy suggests the server; some users suggests DNS, a region or a client network.
2. Think it through (the reasoning, step by step)
  1. Say the order out loud. "I walk the request path: DNS, TCP, TLS, HTTP, and stop at the first stage that fails."
  2. Name the command and the signature for each stage. getent hosts (no answer: DNS); nc -zv host 443 (refused: nothing listening; timed out: firewall or routing); openssl s_client (certificate errors); curl -sv (status code and headers; 5xx points at the server or a proxy).
  3. Compare with a working vantage point. Test from another network or machine to separate a client-side problem from a server-side one.
  4. Mitigate if users are hurting. If a recent change correlates, roll it back first; investigate afterwards.
  5. Then go to the server. ss -ltnp for the listener, process state, application and proxy logs, resource saturation (the performance triage module).
3. A strong answer, said out loud

"First I would find out who is affected, since when, and what changed. Then I walk the request path in order and stop at the first failure: does the name resolve with getent hosts; does the port accept connections with nc -zv, where refused means nothing is listening and timed out means a firewall or routing problem; is the certificate valid with openssl s_client; and what does curl -sv return, since a 5xx means the server or a proxy failed.

I would also test from a second network to tell a client problem from a server problem. If a deploy just went out, I would roll it back to stop the harm. Once I know the failing stage, I go to the server: check the listener with ss, the process, application and proxy logs, and resource saturation. Afterwards I would write up what happened and add a check or alert for that failure."

4. Follow-ups you should expect
It works for some users only. What next?

Compare the working and failing users: region, resolver, ISP, client version. That points at DNS caches or geo routing, a single bad backend behind the load balancer, or a partial CDN issue. Check per-backend error rates.

It is slow, not down. How do you find where?

curl -w timings split DNS, TCP, TLS and time to first byte; the biggest gap is the stage to investigate, and a large gap before the first byte points at the server.

How do you make sure you find out before customers do?

Synthetic checks from outside (DNS, TLS expiry, HTTP) and alerts on error rate and latency, so the page arrives before the complaint.

Common slips
  • Reading application logs before confirming the request even arrives.
  • Restarting things before capturing evidence.

Interview corner

Networking answers sound best as a story that walks the stages in order, with a cost and a failure for each. Practise saying the flow out loud; then use the worked scenarios in each lesson as models.

What happens when you type a URL into a browser and press Enter?
Shape of a strong answer
  • Caches first; then DNS: the resolver walks root, TLD and authoritative servers and caches the answer for its TTL.
  • TCP three-way handshake to port 443 (one round trip); TLS handshake (certificate chain verified, session keys derived; one more round trip in TLS 1.3).
  • HTTP request and response (status, headers, body); the browser parses the HTML and fetches more resources, reusing the connection; then render.
  • Cost: about three round trips cold; which is why CDNs, keep-alive and HTTP/2 and 3 matter. Debug by walking the same stages with commands.
Follow-ups to expect
  • Where does a CDN fit in?
  • What changes on a repeat visit?
  • Which stage is usually slowest?

  • Skipping DNS or TLS.
  • Not mentioning caches.

Explain the TCP three-way handshake and what connection refused versus timed out mean.
Shape of a strong answer
  • SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK: both sides agree on initial sequence numbers; one round trip.
  • Refused: the host answered with a reset because nothing listens on that port.
  • Timed out: no answer, usually a firewall drop, bad route or dead host.

  • How would you tell a firewall from a stopped service?
  • What is slow start?
Common mistakes
  • Treating the two errors as the same.
TCP versus UDP: when would you use each?
Shape of a strong answer
  • TCP: reliable, ordered, with flow and congestion control; costs a handshake and head-of-line blocking. UDP: independent datagrams, no guarantees, low latency.
  • TCP for web, SSH, databases; UDP for DNS, voice and video, games, QUIC (reliability built on top).
Follow-ups to expect
  • What is head-of-line blocking?
  • Why does DNS use UDP?
Common mistakes
  • Saying UDP is just worse.
How does DNS work, and why do DNS changes take time to be seen everywhere?
Shape of a strong answer
  • Stub resolver asks a recursive resolver; it walks root, TLD, authoritative and caches the result for the TTL.
  • Records: A, AAAA, CNAME, MX, NS, TXT.
  • Caches everywhere hold old answers until their TTL expires; lower the TTL before a migration.

Common mistakes

What is the difference between HTTP/1.1, HTTP/2 and HTTP/3?
Shape of a strong answer
  • 1.1: one request at a time per connection, keep-alive; HTTP-level head-of-line blocking.
  • 2: multiplexed streams on one TCP connection with header compression; but TCP loss stalls all streams.
  • 3: QUIC over UDP; per-stream loss recovery, faster handshake, survives network changes.

How does HTTPS keep traffic safe? What happens if a certificate is expired?
Shape of a strong answer
  • TLS handshake agrees session keys (confidentiality, integrity) and the client verifies the certificate chain, name and dates (authentication).
