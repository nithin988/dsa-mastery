Measure, alert, respond

Turn "is it reliable?" into a number, spend it on purpose, and page a human only for what users feel.

about 15 minutes4 steps2 real experiments3 interview questions

13:04 a.m., the wrong alarmThe story

The pager says CPU above 80% on web-3. Mira, on call, checks: it is the nightly report job and users are fine. She acknowledges it and goes back to bed. It is the third time this week. Over a month that alert pages 237 times, and almost none of them are about a real user problem.

On Tuesday at 2:10 p.m. a payment provider slows down. For three hours 8 percent of ShopKart's requests fail. No alert fires, because CPU is fine. The first sign is a customer email. When the founder asks "how reliable are we?", nobody can answer with a number.

You do four things. Choose a number users would accept: 99.9 percent of requests good over 30 days. Work out the budget that allows. Alert on how fast the budget is burning instead of on CPU. And time every incident, then write what happened without blaming anyone. The same month would have produced 2 pages and 1 ticket.

2The idea: reliability is a budget you spend on purposeThe idea

Perfect reliability is impossible and expensive. An SLO sets the target, the error budget is how much failure that target allows, and burn rate tells you how fast you are spending it.

This replaces arguments with a shared rule. While there is budget, the team ships and takes risks. When it is spent, risky releases stop until reliability is fixed. Alerts follow the same logic: page when the budget will be gone soon, ticket when it is slipping slowly, and stay quiet about causes that users cannot feel.

3How it works, step by stepHow it works

  1. Measure what users feel (the SLI)

    A service level indicator (SLI) is the share of requests that were good: they succeeded and were fast enough. Measure it where the user is, at the edge, not on a machine: CPU can be at five percent while every payment fails.

  2. Set the target and spend the budget (the SLO)

    A service level objective (SLO) is the target for that share over a window, such as 99.9 percent over 30 days. What it allows to fail is the error budget: 0.1 percent, about 43 minutes of full outage. While there is budget you ship; when it is gone you fix reliability.

  3. Alert on burn rate, over two windows

    The burn rate is how many times faster than the allowed pace the budget is being used. A fast burn pages someone and a slow burn opens a ticket. Checking a long window and a short window together proves the problem is real and still happening.

  4. Run the incident, then write the postmortem

    Time every incident: detect, acknowledge, mitigate, resolve. Mitigate first (roll back or fail over), then find the cause. Afterwards write a blameless postmortem: what happened, why the system allowed it, and follow up actions with owners.

1What a target allows, and what three incidents cost

The arithmetic that makes an SLO usable. 99.9 percent over 30 days leaves about 43 minutes of full outage; 99.99 percent leaves under 4. Counting in requests is fairer for a service whose traffic varies: at three million requests a day, the budget is 90,000 failed requests a month. The incident list shows how quickly a budget disappears: a bad release at 60 percent errors for 40 minutes costs more than half of it, and with a failover and a slow dependency on top, the budget is gone. At that point the agreed rule applies: slow down risky changes and invest in reliability.

WINDOW_MIN = 30 * 24 * 60
print('SLO       error budget          full outage allowed in 30 days')
for slo in (99.0, 99.5, 99.9, 99.95, 99.99):
    budget = 100 - slo
    mins = WINDOW_MIN * budget / 100

2Noisy alerts, burn-rate alerts and an incident clock

Why symptom and burn-rate alerts beat cause alerts. A threshold on CPU fires 237 times in the month, while the three real incidents are a tiny part of what wakes anyone. The burn-rate rules fire only for the three incidents: the 25-minute spike and the 8-minute outage page within minutes (budget burning at a dangerous multiple on both a long and a short window), and the three-hour slow leak, which never burns fast enough to page, is caught by the slower rule as a ticket about two hours in. The incident timeline then shows the numbers worth reporting after the event: how long to detect, acknowledge, mitigate and resolve, and why mitigating first (a rollback in 31 minutes) matters more than the 180 minutes it took to fully fix the cause.

python3 - <<'EOF'
import random
rng = random.Random(21)
SLO_ERR = 0.001                                           # 99.9% SLO: 0.1% of requests may fail
minutes = 30 * 24 * 60
# per-minute error ratio: tiny blips on most minutes, three real incidents
err = [max(0.0, rng.gauss(0.0004, 0.0004)) for _ in range(minutes)]
for start, length, level in ((5000, 25, 0.12), (21000, 8, 0.6), (33000, 180, 0.008)):
    for m in range(start, start + length): err[m] = level
cpu = []
for _ in range(minutes // 10): cpu += [rng.random() < 0.06] * 10   # "CPU above 80%" in about 6% of 10-minute blocks, mostly harmlessly
def naive_alerts():
    fired, quiet = 0, True
    for m in range(minutes):
        if cpu[m] and quiet: fired += 1; quiet = False
        if not cpu[m]: quiet = True
    return fired
def burn(m, window):
    lo = max(0, m - window + 1); return (sum(err[lo:m + 1]) / (m - lo + 1)) / SLO_ERR
def burn_alerts():
    pages, tickets, active_p, active_t, hits, last_page = 0, 0, False, False, [], -10**9
    for m in range(minutes):
        fast = burn(m, 60) >= 14 and burn(m, 5) >= 14           # fast burn: both windows over 14x
        slow = burn(m, 360) >= 3 and burn(m, 30) >= 3           # slow burn: both windows over 3x
        if fast and not active_p: pages += 1; hits.append((m, 'PAGE')); active_p = True
        if fast: last_page = m
        if not fast: active_p = False
        if slow and m - last_page > 360 and not active_t: tickets += 1; hits.append((m, 'ticket')); active_t = True
        if not slow: active_t = False
    return pages, tickets, hits
p, t, hits = burn_alerts()
print('30 days, 3 real incidents (a 25 min spike, an 8 min outage, a 3 hour slow leak)')
print('alert on "CPU above 80%%": %d pages, almost none about a real user problem' % naive_alerts())
print('burn-rate alerts: %d pages and %d tickets' % (p, t))
for m, kind in hits: print('   minute %5d (day %.1f): %s' % (m, m / 1440, kind))
print()
tl = {'incident began': 0, 'alert fired': 4, 'acknowledged': 9, 'mitigated (rolled back)': 31, 'resolved (root cause fixed)': 180}
prev = 0
print('an incident timeline (minutes after the start):')
for k, v in tl.items(): print('   %4d  %s' % (v, k))
print('time to detect %d min, to acknowledge %d min, to mitigate %d min, to resolve %d min' % (tl['alert fired'], tl['acknowledged'] - tl['alert fired'], tl['mitigated (rolled back)'], tl['resolved (root cause fixed)']))
EOF

5How it breaks in productionFailure modes

An SLO of 100 percent

It is impossible, it freezes all change, and users stop noticing after the first few nines. Fix: set the target just above what users notice and use the budget to decide.

SLIs that measure machines

CPU and memory can look healthy while users fail, and the reverse. Fix: measure good requests at the edge: success and speed as the user sees them.

Alerts on causes

A page for every CPU spike teaches people to ignore the pager, so the real alert is late. Fix: page on SLO burn and keep cause metrics on dashboards for diagnosis.

No policy for a spent budget

If nobody agreed what happens when the budget is gone, it is only a chart. Fix: agree in advance with product and engineering: slow risky releases and fund reliability work.

Blaming a person

A postmortem that names a culprit teaches people to hide mistakes. Fix: write what happened, why the system allowed it, and what changes, assuming everyone acted sensibly with what they knew.

Alerts nobody can act on

A page with no dashboard and no runbook wastes the first ten minutes. Fix: every page links to a dashboard and a runbook, and unactionable pages are deleted.

7In the interviewTop-company questions

Say your answer aloud first, then compare with the model answer. These are the shapes of question candidates report, not official lists.

A core SRE question: reported by candidates, not official.

"What is an SLO and how do you use an error budget to make decisions?"

A core SRE interview question. They want definitions tied to decisions.

Ask first

  • Is this a user-facing service? What do users notice most: errors, latency or freshness? Choose the SLI from what users feel.

How to think about it

  1. Define the terms.SLI measures good over total; SLO is the target over a window; the error budget is one minus the SLO; an SLA is a customer contract, looser than the SLO.
  2. Choose SLIs.Availability (successful responses), latency (fast enough, as a percentile threshold), possibly correctness and freshness, measured at the edge.
  3. Choose the target.From user tolerance and cost; avoid 100 percent; set it just above what users notice.
  4. Use the budget.While budget remains, release freely and run experiments; when it is spent, freeze risky changes and prioritise reliability work; the policy is agreed in advance by product and engineering.
  5. Alert on burn rate.Page when the budget is being consumed too fast to last the window.
  6. Review.Look at budget consumption in reviews; tune SLOs with data.

How to say it

A model answer, about two minutes aloud

"An SLI measures what users experience as a ratio, say successful and fast requests over all requests. An SLO is the target for it over a window, for example 99.9 percent over 30 days, and the error budget is what that allows to fail: 0.1 percent, about 43 minutes of full outage a month. An SLA is the looser customer contract.

I use the budget to decide: while there is budget we ship and experiment; when it is spent we freeze risky changes and put the effort into reliability. That rule is agreed in advance by product and engineering, so it is a shared policy and not an argument each time. I alert on how fast the budget is burning rather than on raw errors."

Likely follow-ups

Why not aim for 100 percent?

It is impossible, and each extra nine costs much more while users stop noticing; it would also freeze all change.

Which latency measure do you use?

A percentile threshold such as the share of requests faster than 300 ms; averages hide the tail.

What if the SLO is always met with lots of budget left?

Either the SLO is too loose, or you can ship faster and take more risk.

Common slips

  • Choosing CPU or memory as an SLI.
  • Setting 100 percent.
  • Having an SLO nobody uses to make decisions.
An operations maturity question: reported by candidates, not official.

"Our on-call gets paged 40 times a week and ignores most of it. How do you fix alerting?"

An operations maturity question. They want symptom-based alerts, burn rates, runbooks and a way to measure improvement.

Ask first

  • What do the pages look like, which ones need action, and what are the SLOs? Count pages and the share that needed a human.

How to think about it

  1. Audit the pages.For each alert in the last month: did it need action? Was it a symptom users feel? Delete or downgrade the ones that did not.
  2. Alert on symptoms.Page on SLO burn (error rate and latency at the edge), not on causes like CPU; keep cause metrics on dashboards for diagnosis.
  3. Use burn rates with two windows.Fast burn pages, slow burn tickets, so urgency matches the time the budget has left.
  4. Make every page actionable.Each alert links to a dashboard and a runbook with first checks and mitigations.
  5. Reduce toil.Automate recurring fixes; group duplicates; silence during known maintenance.
  6. Measure.Pages per on-call shift, share actionable, time to acknowledge and mitigate, and incidents first reported by customers.

How to say it

A model answer, about two minutes aloud

"I would start by auditing a month of pages: which ones needed a human, and which reflected something users felt. Alerts on causes like CPU get deleted or turned into dashboards. I would page on SLO burn instead: error rate and latency at the edge, using burn rates over two windows, so a fast burn pages and a slow burn opens a ticket.

Every page must be actionable with a link to a runbook and dashboards, recurring fixes get automated, and duplicates are grouped. I would track pages per shift, the share that were actionable, time to acknowledge and mitigate, and how many incidents customers noticed before we did, and review those numbers each month."

Likely follow-ups

What is a burn rate?

The error ratio divided by the allowed error ratio: 1 would use exactly the whole budget over the window; 14 means it is gone in about two days.

Why two windows?

The long window proves the problem is real; the short one proves it is still happening, so alerts stop soon after recovery.

What goes in a blameless postmortem?

A timeline, impact, root causes in the system and process, what worked, and follow-up actions with owners and dates: no blaming individuals.

Common slips

  • Alerting on every possible cause.
  • Pages with no runbook.
  • Postmortems that name a person and have no follow-ups.
An incident handling question: reported by candidates, not official.

Describe how you would run a production incident.

Amazon-style: expect customer-impact and ownership questions, and a request for a real example with numbers; reported by candidates, not official.

How to say it

A model answer, about two minutes aloud

Detect and triage: scope, severity, a lead and a communication channel.

Mitigate first (rollback, failover, flag off); keep a timeline.

Diagnose with the playbooks and the method; fix and verify the original symptom.

Blameless postmortem with owned actions.

Likely follow-ups

• How do you decide when to roll back?

Common slips

  • Debugging live without mitigating.

Check yourself

What is an error budget?

One minus the SLO: how much failure you may spend in the window.

Why alert on burn rate instead of CPU?

Burn rate tracks what users feel and how soon the budget runs out; CPU alerts fire often and rarely matter.

What does mitigate first mean?

Stop the pain with a rollback or failover before hunting the cause, which is why mitigation can take 31 minutes and the full fix 180.
