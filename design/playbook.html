Part 3The interview playbook

The interview playbook

How to run the 45 minutes, explain your choices, avoid the usual mistakes, and practise in the right order.

about 20 minutes7 sectionsa four-week plan

1What interviewers are looking forThe rubric

Interview-prep sites and engineers who run these interviews describe the same flow, even when they give it different names: clarify the requirements, estimate only what changes the design, define the API and data, draw the high-level design, go deep on the hard part, then cover failures and trade-offs. Senior-level signal is usually scored in the deep dive, so a candidate who runs out of time before it loses the chance to show it.

Scoping

Do you ask what the system must do, for whom, and how big, before you draw?

Structure

Can you break a vague problem into stages and drive the conversation?

Depth

Do you understand how at least one part really works, with numbers?

Trade-offs

Do you say what each choice costs and when you would choose differently?

Failure thinking

Do you ask what happens when this node, queue or region dies?

Communication

Can the interviewer follow you, and do you adapt when they push back?

Sources: Hello Interview delivery framework and a comparison of common frameworks. These are third-party summaries, not official company rubrics.

2The 45 minutes, minute by minuteThe plan

MinutesStageWhat you say and do
0 to 5ClarifyAsk 4 to 6 questions: users, features in and out of scope, scale, latency, consistency. Write the answers down.
5 to 10NumbersOnly the estimates that decide something: requests per second, storage, bandwidth. State the conclusion: "so one cache tier is enough".
10 to 15API and dataThe three or four endpoints and the main entities. Choose the shape of the store and say why.
15 to 30High-level designDraw the flows end to end: the write path, the read path. Name each component and what problem it solves.
30 to 40Deep dive and failurePick the hardest part (or the one they point at) and go deep. Then: what breaks, how you would know, what you would do.
40 to 45Wrap upSummarise the decisions and trade-offs, what you would improve with more time, and how you would monitor it.

3Say it like thisSentences that work

Good answers sound the same in every interview because they follow the same moves. Keep these sentence starters in your head:

Opening

"Before I draw anything, I would like to ask a few questions, then do a quick estimate, then sketch the design, then go deep on the hardest part. Is that a good use of the time?"

Clarifying

"Do we need X? I will assume yes unless you say otherwise. How many users, and how fresh must the data be?"

Estimating

"That is about 12,000 writes a second at peak. One database node handles a few thousand, so we need partitioning from day one."

Choosing

"I would use a cache-aside Redis here because reads are 100 times the writes and a few seconds of staleness is fine."

Trade-off

"The cost of this choice is more complex writes. I would accept that because ... If the requirement were strict consistency, I would instead ..."

Failure

"If this node dies, clients retry on the replica within a few seconds, and we lose at most the last second of data because replication is asynchronous."

Being challenged

"That is a fair point. The constraint I was optimising for was latency. If cost matters more, I would change this part by ..."

4The ten mistakes that cost the mostMistakes

Starting to draw at once

Without requirements you design the wrong system. Ask first.

Technology name-dropping

"Use Kafka" is not a reason. Say what problem it solves here and what it costs.

No numbers

Boxes without numbers cannot be judged. Estimate, then let the numbers choose.

Estimating for 20 minutes

Estimates serve the design. Take the number you need and move on.

A diagram with no flows

Show how one request travels: the write path and the read path.

Equal time on every box

Spend your time where the problem is hard.

Ignoring failure

Every component dies. Say what happens and how you would notice.

Defending instead of exploring

A challenge is information about their constraint. Ask what it is.

Premature scale

Start simple, then show where and why it must grow.

Forgetting to summarise

Finish with the decisions, the trade-offs and what you would do next.

6A four-week practice planPractice

Read a page, run its experiments, then say the model answer aloud without looking, timed. Reading is not practising; speaking is.

WeekFocusDo this
1The building blocksChapters 1 to 5. After each, explain the block to a friend in two minutes: what broke, what it does, what it costs.
2Failure and operationsChapters 6 to 9. For each, write three failure scenarios and what you would do.
3Classic problemsProblems 1 to 4 and 8. Time yourself: 45 minutes, whiteboard or paper, then compare with the page.
4Harder problems and mocksProblems 5 to 7, 9, 10. Do two full mock interviews with a friend, who should interrupt with the follow-ups listed on each page.

7Are you ready? A self-checkChecklist

  • I can turn "a million users" into requests per second and storage in under a minute.
  • For each building block I can say what problem it solves, how it works and how it breaks.
  • I always ask clarifying questions and write down the answers.
  • I can draw the write path and the read path for any of the ten problems.
  • I can state at least two trade-offs for every choice I make.
  • I know what happens when each component fails, and how I would notice.
  • I can explain a design clearly in two minutes and in forty-five.
