Design a notification system

Sending a notification is easy. The system is the part that decides whether to send, merges the noise, retries safely and never lets marketing delay a password reset.

about 30 minutes3 real experimentsReported for apps with push, email and SMS

1A photo that went popularThe question

ShopKart sellers want to know when someone likes their photo or buys something, so the team sends a phone push for every event. It is a loop that calls the push provider. It works for a month.

Then one seller's photo takes off. Over ten minutes it collects 1,000 likes, so the phone buzzes 1,000 times and the seller turns notifications off for the whole app. The same week the email provider has an outage, and a backlog of retries delays a password reset by an hour. The loop needed rules, priorities and a way to give up gracefully.

The interviewer says: Design a notification system for an app with 500 million users: push, email and SMS.

What they are looking for: A pipeline design question. They want ingestion, rules, preferences, prioritisation, delivery and failure handling with numbers.

2What to ask before drawing anythingRequirements

Two minutes of questions is what separates a design from a guess. Write the answers on the board.

  • Types of notifications, volume, latency targets, providers, user preferences and regulations (unsubscribe, SMS consent)? Assume 10 billion notifications a day, transactional ones within seconds, marketing within minutes, strict unsubscribe handling.

Functional: accept events from many services; decide who gets what on which channel (push, email, SMS); respect preferences, quiet hours and unsubscribe; retry failures; record what was sent. Non-functional: urgent messages arrive in seconds; low priority never blocks high priority; nothing is sent twice; the system survives a provider outage.

3The numbers that shape the designEstimates

QuantityEstimate and what it tells you
Assumptions500 million users, 10 billion notifications a day, transactional ones within seconds, marketing within minutes. Confirm these.
Rate10 billion / 86,400 seconds is about 115,000 per second on average, around 500,000 at peak.
A smaller serviceFor 10 million emails a day: about 115 per second on average, perhaps 1,000 at peak. With 100 ms per send and 50 concurrent senders, capacity is 500 per second, so a queue must smooth the peak.
CostThe provider call dominates cost, especially SMS. Collapsing and caps save money as well as annoyance.
PresenceDo not send a push to someone actively in the app. A short lived 'online' key per user answers that question cheaply.

11,000 likes, quiet hours and a per user limit

Why notification systems are mostly about restraint. Without collapsing, 1,000 likes become 1,000 pushes (limited only by the per-user cap, and the user is buried); with collapsing, they become one message ("user0 and 999 others liked your photo"). The pipeline also holds back marketing and routine events during quiet hours while letting the urgent password reset through, and a per-user rate limit caps what remains. Every rule is a product decision as well as an engineering one: the metric that matters is not messages sent but messages that were useful.

# A notification pipeline in miniature: 1,000 likes on one photo, a few other events, quiet hours and a per-user limit.
from collections import defaultdict
events = [('like', 'ana', 'photo1', 'user%d' % i, 9) for i in range(1000)]            # 1,000 likes between 09:00 and 09:10
events += [('comment', 'ana', 'photo1', 'ben', 9), ('password_reset', 'ana', None, 'system', 3), ('marketing', 'ana', None, 'system', 3), ('mention', 'ana', 'post9', 'cho', 3)]
QUIET = range(0, 7)                                                                  # no pushes between 00:00 and 07:00 unless urgent
URGENT = {'password_reset'}
def pipeline(events, collapse=True, limit=5):
    sent, held, collapsed = [], [], 0
    likes = [e for e in events if e[0] == 'like']
    rest = [e for e in events if e[0] != 'like']
    out = []
    if collapse and likes: out.append(('like', 'ana', 'photo1', '%s and %d others' % (likes[0][3], len(likes) - 1), likes[0][4])); collapsed = len(likes) - 1
    else: out += likes
    out += rest
    per_hour = defaultdict(int)
    for kind, user, obj, actor, hour in out:
        if hour in QUIET and kind not in URGENT: held.append((kind, actor)); continue         # deferred to the morning digest
        if per_hour[hour] >= limit and kind not in URGENT: held.append((kind, actor)); continue   # per-user rate limit
        per_hour[hour] += 1; sent.append((kind, actor, hour))
    return sent, held, collapsed
for collapse in (False, True):
    sent, held, c = pipeline(events, collapse=collapse)
    print('collapsing %-5s: %4d pushes sent, %4d held back (rate limit / quiet hours)%s' % (collapse, len(sent), len(held), ('; %d likes merged into one' % c) if c else ''))
sent, held, _ = pipeline(events, True)
print('sent:', [(k, a) for k, a, h in sent])
print('held for the morning digest or limits:', held)
EOF

The same code runs on any Debian or Ubuntu machine as an ordinary user; nothing needs root.

4A first design that worksArchitecture

  1. Events go on a queue, never straight to a provider

    Services publish events like 'order shipped' or 'like received'. The notification service reads them from a queue, so a spike waits in line instead of overwhelming the senders.

  2. Rules decide whether, when and where

    A rules step checks the user's channel choices, quiet hours, frequency cap and consent. Repeated events with the same user, type and object inside a window are merged into one, like '1,000 likes' becoming a single line.

  3. Separate lanes for urgent and casual

    Each priority has its own queue and its own workers. A password reset sits in the fast lane, and a newsletter in a slow lane that is rate limited. When the system is busy, the casual lane waits first.

  4. Retry with care, then give up loudly

    A failed send is retried after a growing, jittered delay, with an idempotency key so a retry cannot double send. A message that keeps failing, such as a malformed address, moves to a dead letter queue where a person can inspect it.

5The hard part: not annoying people, and not falling overThe hard part

Every interview has one part where the answer is not obvious. Spend your time here.

Two things make notifications hard. The first is volume you did not ask for: collapse repeats, cap per user, and hold low priority messages for quiet hours. The second is failure: providers are slow or down, so retries, back pressure and priority lanes keep the important messages moving.

Presence is a small trick worth knowing. A user's 'online' key expires on its own a few seconds after the heartbeats stop, so nobody has to clean up. The experiments below show the retry and dead letter flow, then presence.

1Retry with backoff, then the dead letter queue

Retries done properly. m2 fails once with a transient timeout, waits (a delay that doubles with random jitter) and succeeds on the second attempt. m3 is a poison message: it fails every time, so after the attempt limit it is moved to the dead-letter queue with its error, instead of blocking the others or retrying forever. m4 is processed normally afterwards. The DLQ must be monitored: a growing DLQ is a bug report, and its messages can be fixed and replayed.

python3 - <<'EOF'
import random, time
rng = random.Random(1)
dlq, log = [], []
def handle(msg, attempt):
    if msg.get('bad'): raise ValueError('malformed payload')               # a poison message: will never succeed
    if msg['id'] == 'm2' and attempt == 1: raise TimeoutError('downstream timed out')   # transient: works on retry
def process(msg, max_attempts=4, base=0.05):
    for attempt in range(1, max_attempts + 1):
        try:
            handle(msg, attempt); log.append('%s attempt %d: ok' % (msg['id'], attempt)); return
        except Exception as e:
            log.append('%s attempt %d: %s' % (msg['id'], attempt, e))
            if attempt == max_attempts:
                dlq.append((msg['id'], str(e))); log.append('%s -> dead-letter queue' % msg['id']); return
            delay = base * 2 ** (attempt - 1) * (0.5 + rng.random())      # exponential backoff with jitter
            log.append('   waiting %.0f ms before retry' % (delay * 1000)); time.sleep(delay)
for m in ({'id': 'm1'}, {'id': 'm2'}, {'id': 'm3', 'bad': True}, {'id': 'm4'}): process(m)
print('\n'.join(log)); print('dead-letter queue now holds:', dlq)

2A bounded queue protects the workers

Back-pressure, measured. Jobs arrive at about 100 per second but are processed at 50 per second. The unbounded queue accepts everything, so its depth keeps growing (about 50 more jobs every second) and a newly arrived job would wait many seconds: latency grows and memory fills while every job is still "accepted". The bounded queue holds at most 20, so the excess is rejected immediately (the caller gets a clear busy and can retry later or degrade), and the wait for accepted jobs stays under half a second. Rejecting early is usually kinder than accepting work you cannot finish.

python3 - <<'EOF'
import queue, threading, time
def scenario(maxsize, label):
    q = queue.Queue(maxsize=maxsize); stats = {'done': 0, 'rejected': 0, 'peak': 0}
    stop = threading.Event()
    def worker():
        while not stop.is_set():
            try: q.get(timeout=0.1)
            except queue.Empty: continue
            time.sleep(0.02); stats['done'] += 1                          # service rate: 50 per second
    t = threading.Thread(target=worker); t.start()
    start = time.time()
    while time.time() - start < 3:                                        # arrival rate: 100 per second for 3 seconds
        try: q.put_nowait(1)
        except queue.Full: stats['rejected'] += 1
        stats['peak'] = max(stats['peak'], q.qsize()); time.sleep(0.01)
    wait = q.qsize() * 0.02
    stop.set(); t.join()
    print('%-22s peak depth %3d, rejected %3d, a new job would now wait about %.1f s' % (label, stats['peak'], stats['rejected'], wait))
scenario(0, 'unbounded queue:')
scenario(20, 'bounded queue (20):')

3Presence that expires by itself

Presence in four commands. A heartbeat is a SET with an expiry; a user who stops sending heartbeats (closed app, dead battery, lost network) disappears when the key expires, with no logout event and no cleanup job. Ana keeps refreshing her key and stays online; Ben and Cho lapse by themselves. The same pattern is used for locks, sessions and leases: let the absence of a signal mean "gone". The accuracy is bounded by the expiry (a crash looks online for up to a few seconds), which is a deliberate trade-off.

6Scale, failure and trade-offsDeep dive

A provider is down

Open a circuit breaker so workers stop hammering it, switch to a second provider where you have one, and let the queue hold the rest.

Retry storms

Without jitter, thousands of retries land at the same moment and knock the provider over again. Add random delay and a cap on attempts.

Duplicate sends

A worker crashes after sending but before recording it. Use an idempotency key so the retry is recognised and skipped.

Dead tokens

Push tokens go stale when people uninstall. Remove tokens the provider rejects so you stop paying to send into the void.

Compliance

Honour unsubscribe and SMS consent instantly, and keep an audit trail of what was sent and why.

3. How to say it

A model answer, about two minutes aloud

"Ten billion notifications a day is about 115,000 per second on average and perhaps 500,000 at peak, so the pipeline is queue-based. Services publish events; a notification service consumes them, looks up the user's preferences, quiet hours, frequency caps and consent in a fast store, and decides whether, when and through which channel to notify. It deduplicates and collapses repeated events into digests and uses idempotency keys so retries never double-send.

Work is split into priority queues with separate worker pools, so security and transactional messages are never delayed by marketing, which is rate limited and scheduled. Provider adapters for push, email and SMS handle retries with backoff, per-provider rate limits, circuit breakers and failover to a second provider, remove invalid tokens and record delivery receipts. I would track delivery, open and unsubscribe rates, alert on backlog by priority, keep a dead-letter queue and an audit trail for compliance, and make sure transactional notifications are never lost and never sent twice."

4. Likely follow-ups

How do you prevent notification fatigue?

Collapsing, frequency caps, quiet hours, relevance scoring and easy per-type opt-outs; measure unsubscribes and treat them as an error budget.

What if the push provider is down?

Retry with backoff, fail over to another channel for critical messages (SMS or email), and queue the rest; monitor provider health with a circuit breaker.

How do you handle time zones?

Store the user's time zone and quiet hours; schedule marketing in local time with jitter to avoid spikes.

Common slips

  • One queue for all priorities.
  • No deduplication or idempotency.
  • Treating push delivery as guaranteed.

8Remember thisRecap

The page in 6 lines

  • Put events on a queue, so spikes wait instead of crashing senders.
  • Rules step: preferences, quiet hours, frequency caps, consent.
  • Collapse repeats: 1,000 likes become one notification.
  • Priority lanes so urgent messages never wait behind marketing.
  • Retry with backoff and jitter, with an idempotency key, then dead letter.
  • Presence is a key with an expiry, refreshed by heartbeats.

Check yourself

Why collapse notifications?

Repeated events annoy people and cost money. In the experiment 999 likes merged into one push.

What is a dead letter queue?

A holding place for messages that keep failing, so they stop blocking the queue and a person can inspect them.

Why add jitter to retry delays?

So many failed messages do not all retry at the same instant and overload the provider again.
